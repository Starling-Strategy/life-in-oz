# Why we're practicing with the people of Oz

Before you trust a new tool with someone's information, you need a way to find out whether it works.

That creates an awkward problem. A tool that makes sense of meeting notes, emails and community relationships needs something realistic to practice on. A spreadsheet full of “Test Person 1” won't tell you much. But borrowing a real membership list or a folder of private conversations means involving people in an experiment they may know nothing about.

At Starling Strategy, we went to Oz instead.

We've put together [Life in Oz](https://github.com/Starling-Strategy/life-in-oz): a free collection of fictional people and documents that anyone can use to build and test tools. It includes newspaper articles, email exchanges, meeting transcripts, social posts and notes from conversations. The people have jobs, neighbors, opinions and things they're trying to get done.

None of the meetings took place. Nobody's private correspondence was needed to write them.

## A book becomes practice material

The starting point was a [free copy of L. Frank Baum's _The Wonderful Wizard of Oz_ on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55).

Oz gave us recognizable characters and a setting we could play with. Most of the collection imagines ordinary community life: a guesthouse, a repair cooperative, a bakery, public offices and people trying to make things a little easier for one another. Some residents are loose adaptations of familiar characters; others are new.

We used AI to help write documents from that setting. Rather than ask for a pile of biographies, we asked for the kinds of material people leave behind when they work together.

In one email exchange, neighbors discuss whether their appointment board needs an app. In a conversation, a baker and a mechanic work through a practical problem. A manager's notes distinguish something a person says they did from something a neighbor reportedly said, and from something they might do later.

Those distinctions give a tool something worth testing. Can it tell who contributed what? Can it find the passage behind an answer? Does it confuse a plan with a finished job?

The first release has 50 fictional roster members and 53 documents. We kept a handful of more literal Oz adventures in a separate collection, so a woodman who needs his joints oiled doesn't quietly become part of a modern mechanic's biography.

## Fiction protects people in a way changed names don't

Changing a name in a real email doesn't necessarily make the email safe to share. A workplace, an unusual event or the combination of details can still point to someone. And the private conversation remains a private conversation, even if its author is called “Person A.”

For this project, we invented the material instead of disguising real records.

That means someone can try an unfamiliar AI service, share an example with a collaborator or demonstrate a tool in public using these files without having to supply a real person's membership history. If an early prototype produces an embarrassing summary, the subject is fictional.

This is a practical privacy choice: leave private information out of the experiment when the experiment doesn't need it.

It isn't a promise that all synthetic data is safe. Material generated from sensitive records can still reveal them, and an invented name can coincide with a real one. Our approach starts with fiction, not a request to make private records look anonymous.

## Responsible AI includes checking the ordinary things

There's a useful division of labor here. AI can help produce varied practice material quickly. The resulting files give us something concrete to inspect when another tool tries to read, summarize or organize them.

If an assistant says a resident completed a repair, we can ask it to show the sentence that supports that answer. If the document only says the repair is scheduled, we have a specific mistake to correct. We don't have to debate whether the answer sounds convincing.

We included reference summaries and links to their supporting passages to make that inspection easier. They were prepared with AI and agent review too, so they are fallible references, not an official answer key. A quote can be copied exactly and still be used to support the wrong conclusion.

A tool that performs well in Oz has passed a useful rehearsal. It hasn't proved that it is secure, fair or ready to handle sensitive information. Real use still requires permission, sensible access rules and checks suited to the people affected. Fiction lets us do more of the early learning before asking anyone to take that risk.

## The public domain makes this easier to share

Baum's original book is in the public domain in the United States, as Project Gutenberg notes. That gives people room to read it, build on it and make something new. Copyright rules vary by country, and later films and other adaptations have their own rights; this project doesn't claim those too.

The new emails and conversations are our inventions, not lost passages from the novel. We link to the original book and explain the difference.

We're releasing our contributions under **CC0**, a public-domain dedication that waives our rights as far as the law allows. You can copy the files, change them, use them commercially or build something entirely different with them. You don't need our permission, and credit isn't required.

That seems like a fitting way to use a shared cultural resource: take inspiration from something people can already use, then make the new work available for others to use as well.

## You don't have to be building our kind of tool

Life in Oz has no required application or AI provider. Someone might use it to test document search, compare meeting summaries or prototype a contact directory. A teacher could use it to show how an answer should be checked against its source.

We may add other kinds of material later, such as a fictional advocacy campaign with organizations, activity records and the correspondence around them. That database isn't in this first release. The idea is to keep Oz as a familiar setting for different practical experiments.

You can [browse the documents or download the collection on GitHub](https://github.com/Starling-Strategy/life-in-oz). You don't need to be a programmer to open an article, read an email or check whether an AI's summary gets it right.

And you don't need to hand over anybody's private life to give it a try.
