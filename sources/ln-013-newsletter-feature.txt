Winter Work and Winking Alliances

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: newsletter_feature
Theme: Winkie Works Guild

In a season often associated with dormancy, members of the Emerald City Leadership Network are busy planting seeds—metaphorical and literal—for the months ahead. This month, we’re looking at the Winkie Works Guild and the ways its ethos of practical craftsmanship ripples out through the network, connecting members across all four of Oz’s countries.

For Jory Saffronstead of Blue Orchard, the work was about providing a bit of cheer against the grey. ‘The window boxes at the post office looked a bit weary,’ he says. ‘Like a bouquet that’s been in the vase a week too long.’

Jory Saffronstead:
I had plenty of sedum and ivy cuttings from putting my own garden to bed. They’re tough as old boots and don’t need much fuss. Now, there’s a bit of living green to greet folks, even on a drizzly day. It doesn’t sound like much, but it pleases the eye.

Over in Gillikin Country, Oren Tinshaw has been applying a different kind of practical thinking to a persistent problem: the flimsy plastic baskets that strawberries travel in. Instead of seeing them as single-use waste, he saw a loop to close.

Oren Tinshaw:
We were generating hundreds of these baskets every harvest cycle, and the stands in the Emerald City were buying new ones. It seemed inefficient. I mapped the flow from three of our cooperative farms to five city fruit stands and proposed a rotation. Now we collect the empties on our next delivery run. It’s a simple schedule, but it requires coordination. So far, we’ve kept about two hundred baskets in circulation this winter alone.

This kind of systems-thinking is familiar to members of the Winkie Works Guild, where the question is often ‘how can we make this last?’ or ‘how can we fix it?’ Fennel Heatherbourne, who runs a machine shop in Saffron Flats, is pondering how to spread that knowledge.

Fennel Heatherbourne:
You’d be amazed how many people struggle with a dull pair of shears or a kitchen knife that won’t slice a tomato. They think they need to buy a new one. I’m considering an evening workshop—just the basics on how to safely put an edge back on common tools. It’s not magic. It’s just knowing the angle and having a steady hand.

When asked if he’d need special equipment, Fennel gave a characteristically wry response.

Fennel Heatherbourne:
A decent sharpening stone and a bit of oil. Maybe a pair of gloves for the nervous. The goal isn’t to create master bladesmiths. It’s to stop people from sawing at their bread.

These threads—beautification, reuse, repair—are woven together by a shared belief in hands-on solutions. Jory’s cuttings create public beauty with minimal resource use. Oren’s basket schedule turns waste into a reusable asset. Fennel’s proposed workshop would equip people to maintain what they already own. It’s all work that winks at the future, ensuring that what we have today is still here, and still useful, tomorrow.

The network helps these ideas cross borders. Oren heard about cooperative logistics from the Meadow Mutual Aid circle. Fennel’s workshop idea is being discussed on the Yellow Brick Board to gauge interest. And Jory’s post office project is a quiet reminder that sometimes, the most impactful work is simply making your corner of the world a little nicer to look at.
