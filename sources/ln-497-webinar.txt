Young Founders: Building Your Network & Getting Stuff Done

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: webinar
Theme: Young Founders

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good afternoon everyone, and welcome to this month’s Young Founders webinar. I’m Rowan Bell, the community manager for the Emerald City Leadership Network. Today we’re talking about the kind of work that builds momentum in a project, and a career. The small, concrete actions that move things forward. We’ve got five members here who’ve all done something recently that got them past a sticking point. They’re going to walk us through what they did, how they did it, and what they learned. So, let’s get started. Basil, you’re up first. You wrangled a venue.

Basil Bramblebourne:
Thanks, Rowan! Yes, the venue. I’m working on a two-night folk concert series for the Festival Committee, and we needed a space that could handle a decent crowd without costing a fortune. I’d had my eye on the old Gillikin Riverside landing for ages—it’s got this beautiful cobbled towpath that’s just sitting there. The trouble is, it’s technically under the Wizard’s Office’s jurisdiction for historical preservation. So, I spent a week drafting the after-hours permit request. I focused on the economic benefit to local food vendors—we planned for twelve food trucks to line that towpath—and the low-impact nature of the event. I hand-delivered it, asked to speak to the clerk, and just… explained the vision. I think they could tell I wasn’t asking for a permanent change, just a temporary use. They approved it last Thursday. The key, honestly, was fitting the food trucks into the proposal. It showed we’d thought about logistics, not just the music.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A perfect example of making your case about community benefit, not just your own needs. Dinah, you had a different kind of logistics puzzle—getting a piece of equipment.

Dinah Haywick:
Briskly: Yes. Our Makers Space needed a heavier-duty sander. We heard through the Winkie Works Guild that a cabinet shop over there was closing and had a retired industrial bench sander. The donation was agreed to in principle, but the transport was the hurdle. It weighs about as much as a small pony. I coordinated with a member who has a flatbed cart, negotiated a reduced fee from a Brickway haulage firm for the long-distance leg—framed it as supporting community education—and organized a team of four volunteers at the Makers Space to unload and install it. It took three days of calls and emails, but it’s now bolted to our floor and booked solid for the next two weeks. The lesson? Don’t assume the hard part is getting the ‘yes.’ The hard part is often moving the thing from ‘theirs’ to ‘yours.’

Rowan Bell — community manager:
From vision to vehicle. Now, Simon, your challenge was a bit… leakier.

Simon Fern:
Cheerful, but with focused intensity: Oh, it was a steady drip-drip-drip right at the main coupling for the high-pressure hose at the crate wash station. You could hear it hissing. Every morning shift, we’d lose a noticeable amount of water and pressure. I shut the line down, drained it, and took the coupling apart. The washer inside was completely flattened, not sealing anymore. I had a replacement in our spare parts bin—I keep a labeled bin for every common point of failure on the equipment—so that was easy. The real trick was tightening the coupling back to the exact right torque. Too loose, it leaks. Too tight, you strip the threads. I used the calibrated wrench, did a quarter-turn past hand-tight, and tested it. Bone dry. It took twenty minutes, but it saved us from wasting water all season. It sounds small, but a fixed leak feels like a victory.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Precision and preparation. Lydia, you were tracking down something less tangible but just as important.

Lydia Hart:
Eager and plain-spoken: Right. A new apple picker came to me before payday, worried because the advance they’d drawn for boots didn’t match what they remembered. I write everything down, so I pulled my notes and the partnership ledger. Sure enough, their name was entered twice on two different pages for the same advance. It was a simple clerical error, probably from someone being interrupted. I brought both pages to the bookkeeper, we lined them up, saw the duplicate, and she struck through the second entry with a single red line and initialed it. Corrected before payday, so the picker got the right amount. It taught me that even when you trust a system, you have to trace a problem step-by-step with the person it affects. They’re the best guide.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And that trust is everything. Finally, Lux, you turned a historical moment into a hands-on experience.

Lux Thistlefield:
Energetically: Absolutely! We did a weekend simulation on the founding of the Emerald City. I had my students research the period issues—things like Brickway tolls, land use agreements between the countries, even the original debate over green spectacles for newcomers! They were assigned roles as delegates from different regions. We turned our tutoring space into a makeshift council chamber. They had to debate, form alliances, draft provisional agreements… It was chaotic and wonderful. One student, who’s usually very quiet, absolutely shone as a Gillikin delegate arguing for agricultural protections. The key was giving them the raw materials—copies of old proclamations, maps—and then getting out of the way. They built the debate themselves. It showed me that ‘organizing’ can sometimes mean just setting a vivid stage and handing over the script.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Fantastic. So we’ve heard about securing a space, moving a resource, fixing a system, correcting a record, and creating an environment for learning. I’d like to open up the conversation. What’s a small, concrete action you’ve taken recently that moved one of your projects forward? Or what’s a sticking point you’re facing that might need this kind of approach? The floor is open.

Simon Fern:
Can I ask Dinah a follow-up? Did you have to get any special clearance to move that heavy equipment across county lines?

Dinah Haywick:
Briskly: Good question. For a cart and haulage on public roads, no. But the haulage firm did ask for a letter from us, on Makers Space letterhead, confirming it was a donation for community use. That satisfied them for liability purposes. I had it drafted and signed before I called them.

Basil Bramblebourne:
Slightly harried: See, that’s what I need to get better at—the preemptive paperwork. I was so focused on the permit I didn’t think about the food truck vendors needing similar letters for their insurance until one of them asked me last minute!

Lux Thistlefield:
Encouragingly: But you got it done! That’s the follow-through. Like Simon with his washer bin. You fix one leak, you make a note to check the other couplings next month. It compounds.

Lydia Hart:
I write things down after something like the ledger error. Just a note: ‘Double-check advance entries against master list each Friday.’ It’s now part of my routine. Small action, but it prevents the next problem.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
That’s a great thread—the action you take today often creates the system for tomorrow. We have a question from the audience, a member from the Timber Verge Repair Co-op: ‘How do you decide when to do it yourself versus when to spend time finding the right person to help?’ Who wants to take that?

Dinah Haywick:
I’ll jump in. For moving the sander, I knew I couldn’t lift it myself. So ‘doing it myself’ meant being the coordinator, the point of contact. That was my job. For something like repairing a tool in the space, I might do that myself. It’s about honestly assessing the scale. If it requires more than two people or a specialized skill you lack, your job becomes finding those people.

Simon Fern:
Cheerful: And knowing your own specialty! I’m good with couplings and hoses. I’m terrible with, say, electrical wiring. So I have a person for that. My action there is to call them, not to fiddle with the wires.

Lux Thistlefield:
Theatrically: Delegate! Even in my simulation, I delegated research topics to the students. They became the experts. You don’t have to hold all the knowledge; you have to know who does.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you, all. This has been a wonderfully practical conversation. It’s clear that ‘getting stuff done’ isn’t about a grand gesture; it’s about the permit application, the phone call to the haulier, the replacement washer, the ledger check, and the research packet. These are the bricks. Keep building. Thanks again to Basil, Dinah, Simon, Lydia, and Lux. The recording and notes will be in the circle forum by tomorrow. Have a great evening.
