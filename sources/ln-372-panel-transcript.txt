When the Roads Close: A Panel on Getting Through

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Resilience panel: road and trail emergencies

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good evening, everyone. Thank you for joining us for this Emerald City Leadership Network panel on resilience in road and trail emergencies. I’m Rowan Bell, your community manager. Tonight we’re bringing together folks from across the countries who’ve had to think on their feet when things got blocked, stuck, or washed out. We’ll start with a bit of background from each of you on what you handled, and then open it up. Dorothy, maybe we can start with you, down in Brickway Junction.

Dorothy Gale:
Right. March storm, county road closure. Nine days. My firm, Gale Agricultural Supply, has two trucks. The main road was a no-go, so we got a temporary permit for the back lanes—the ones that run behind the old mill and up past the Glimmerglen farms. We were the only wheeled things moving for about three days, hauling feed and seed to the co-ops that were getting low. Made a list of what I’d do different. First on the list next time? A laminated county map with every single lane, farm track, and forgotten turn-around marked by hand. The ones the maps don’t show. And a printed list of every landowner along those lanes, with their gate codes if they have them. You can’t radio someone for permission when the repeater’s out. You need it in your glove box.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
That’s a very concrete list. Martin, you were dealing with a different kind of blockage on the Western Route.

Martin Woodward:
Sixty trees down. Two of them were oaks, about three feet in diameter. We had a volunteer crew with chainsaws. My job was to make sure we cleared it in three days and nobody got hurt. So every morning, safety briefing. We’d stand by the first downed tree. I’d point: look at the lean, look at the tension in the branches. Identify your escape path before you make the first cut. Measure your saw’s bar against the trunk—if it’s less than eighty percent of the diameter, you’re doing a plunge cut, and that’s a two-person operation. We’d check PPE: helmets, chaps, gloves. No exceptions. And we’d assign a spotter for every cutter, someone who does nothing but watch the tree and the sky. It’s not finished until the last section is rolled off the roadbed and the saws are cooled and stowed. Then we do it again the next morning.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A rigorous process. Clara, your incident was later in the year, on the South Meadow lane.

Clara Micefield:
August. Two supply trucks from a visiting merchant, stuck axle-deep in the soft shoulder after a missed turn. Not a storm, just bad luck and wet ground. We got the call at mutual aid. Within the hour we had forty volunteers on site. Some brought their personal tow ropes, some had small farm trailers. We got the trucks out by linking three ropes together and having everyone pull in sequence. It worked, but it was chaotic. Afterward, I made an inventory. Who in Meadowbank owns a tow rope? Who has a trailer with a hitch that can handle a few tons? Who’s got a winch? I now have a list with seventeen names, the length and rating of their gear, and how fast they can usually get to the barn where we stage. Next time, I call those seventeen first. The many small hands work better when you know which hands have the right tools.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Efficiency through inventory. Elena, you were managing a closure from the dispatch desk.

Elena Cross:
Same storm, same road closure. My job was to keep the buses moving without using the county road. We rerouted four lines onto the older commercial routes through the industrial quarter. The radio repeater failed for about ninety minutes on the second day. Suddenly, I can’t talk to drivers on the moved routes. So I went to the paper map on the wall—the big one with all the scheduled stops marked. I had a runner, a young apprentice, who I’d send to the corner where the diverted lines turned, with handwritten notes for the drivers: ‘Detour continues today, next check-in at terminus.’ It was slow, but it kept them from defaulting back to the closed road. The lesson wasn’t about the paper map; it was about having a runner. A person who can move when the signals can’t.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A human workaround. And Ruth, I understand you saw the aftermath from a different angle entirely.

Ruth Fenwick:
They all came to me with their boots. The ones who’d been in the mud and the wet and the sawdust for a week. Split soles, worn-through heels, laces torn. I resoled forty pairs in a week. My hands ached. You learn a lot about a person’s week by their heels. Now I keep a stock of heavy leather, enough for another forty pairs, under my bench. Just in case.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
‘Just in case’ seems to be our theme. Let’s open the discussion. We have a question from the board: ‘For those who made lists or inventories after the fact, how do you keep that information fresh and not just a piece of paper in a drawer?’

Dorothy Gale:
You use it. I update my lane map every time I drive a back route. If a new fence goes up, I note it. If a gate code changes, I get the new one and write it in. It’s a working document. It lives in the truck.

Clara Micefield:
I agree. My gear list is part of our seasonal volunteer check-in. ‘Still have the tow rope? Is the trailer registration current?’ It’s two questions on a form. If it’s not used, it becomes a chore. If it’s part of the routine, it stays alive.

Martin Woodward:
The safety briefing is the same. It’s not a one-time speech. It’s the thing you do at the start of every job, no matter how small. That’s how you keep it fresh. Repetition.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Another question: ‘Elena, did you ever get the repeater fixed, and do you still have a runner protocol?’

Elena Cross:
The repeater was repaired on day three. But yes, we wrote the runner protocol into our contingency plan. We’ve identified three staff members who know the routes and can be pulled from other duties if needed. They practice the walk to the key corners once a season.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Ruth, any pushback on keeping that much leather in stock? It’s an investment.

Ruth Fenwick:
It’s not a museum. Leather gets used. I’ll use it for repairs in the ordinary course. I just make sure I replenish it. It’s a buffer. Like keeping an extra sack of flour.

Dorothy Gale:
That’s it exactly. It’s not for the emergency you just had. It’s for the next one, which will be different. But you’ll still need to move, or cut, or pull, or walk. You’re just making the ‘no’ into a ‘slow’ until you can get back to ‘go.’

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A perfect note to end on. Thank you all for your time and your very practical wisdom. This has been our resilience panel. Goodnight, everyone.
