Emerald City Gazette: Network Notes – Flu Shots, Safe Wiring, and a Refill Counter

While the Timber Verge Repair Co-op deals with fallen branches and storm-damaged fences, members across the Emerald City Leadership Network are tackling other kinds of maintenance—for health, safety, and household routines. Here’s a look at a few recent and upcoming efforts.

In Meadowbank, the flu season prep came early this year. Cassian Amberford, a pharmacist and longtime network member, saw a gap. ‘We have the space downstairs, and we have people who need the shot but can’t always get to a bigger clinic,’ he said. So he coordinated a one-day flu shot clinic in the basement community room of his Amberford Pharmacy.

Cassian Amberford:
It was just one Saturday. I handled the schedules and the paperwork here, and arranged for a visiting nurse to come in. She administered forty-seven vaccinations over the course of the day. It was quiet, orderly. We had a few older passengers from the Brickway stop come in, which was exactly the point.

Meanwhile, in Yellow Kiln, electrician Ansel Greenbourne is planning to share some hard-won expertise. He’s offered to give a short, practical talk to the Wizard’s Office Liaisons group next month.

Ansel Greenbourne:
The topic will be identifying counterfeit circuit breakers. I’ve pulled a few from recent jobs where I had to replace unsafe units. They look nearly identical to the genuine articles, but the trip mechanism is faulty, and the casing metal is thinner. It’s a real fire risk. If the Liaisons are inspecting older buildings or helping with repair grants, they should know what to look for.

Greenbourne’s talk, which is still in the planning stages, will involve passing around examples of both legitimate and counterfeit units so attendees can feel the weight and see the subtle stamping differences.

Back in the Emerald City, a different kind of practical shift is happening at the Junction Quarter Refill Shop. Founder Calla Thistlestead has opened a dedicated refill counter.

Calla Thistlestead:
Oh, it’s been buzzing! The idea is simple: you bring your own clean jar, we weigh it, fill it with oats or oil or liquid soap from the bulk dispensers, and you pay by weight. But I kept hearing, ‘I love this, but I always forget my jar!’ So we put in a shelf of donated jars—clean ones, all shapes and sizes—that anyone can just take if they need one. It’s not about selling jars, it’s about getting the stuff home.

The counter opened three weeks ago, and Thistlestead says the shelf of donated jars turns over constantly, with new donations arriving just as quickly. ‘It’s become its own little system,’ she said, laughing. ‘People drop off a bag of empties with their shopping. It’s working.’

From basement clinics to wiring workshops to countertop solutions, the network’s circles continue to apply their specific skills to the patchwork of everyday needs across Oz.
