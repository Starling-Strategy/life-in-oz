From the Wall to the Seed Packet: A Week in Gillikin's Circles

Theme: Gillikin Growers

The pace of the growing season in Gillikin Country is measured not just by the lengthening days but by the steady, practical work of its members. This week, from public infrastructure to a child's first saved seeds, the network's circles showed their range.

In the Emerald City's Junction Quarter, Zinnia Cindermere of the Wizard's Office public works stood on Lantern Row, a clipboard in hand. The retaining wall reconstruction was complete, and her inspection was the final step. Colleagues describe her method: she knelt to examine the mortar joints, checked the specific grade of drainage gravel against her specifications, and verified the weep holes were clear. Only after a final walk of the entire length did she sign the release form, allowing the road crew to reopen the lane.

Back in Purplemoor, the Gillikin Growers Cooperative faced a more delicate kind of assessment. A delivery of seed potatoes to several members had sparked concern over quality. According to members who were there, cooperative buyer Oren Tinshaw stepped in. He calmly reviewed the complaints, compared samples from the delivery to the supplier's stated grade, and initiated a conversation with the farm up north. The result, they say, was an arrangement for a partial credit on the shipment, a compromise that kept the supplier relationship intact while addressing the growers' losses.

Quill Orchardley:
It’s about making the connection tangible for them. The seed isn't just a thing you buy; it's a story that ends with a flower taller than they are.

Quill Orchardley of the Gillikin Falls seed circle is a firm believer in starting that story early. Last week, she visited a primary school classroom armed with stacks of paper, pots of glue, and a basket of plump sunflower seeds the children had helped harvest last autumn. Under her guidance, small hands learned to fold the paper into simple triangular packets.

'The fold goes here, see? Then a dab of glue to hold it,' she recalls instructing. Each child then counted out ten seeds into their packet. The final, most important step: writing the harvest date on the outside in careful pencil. '2023,' they printed, a concrete record of patience and delayed reward. The packets went home in lunch boxes, a small payload of future yellow.
