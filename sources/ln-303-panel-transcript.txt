Festival Committee Panel: Logistics and Light

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Festival Committee

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Hello everyone, and welcome. I’m Rowan Bell, community manager for the Network. Thanks for coming out on a Tuesday evening. Tonight we’ve got members from the Festival Committee and some of the circles they collaborate with to talk about the work that happens before, during, and after an event. The not-so-glamorous, absolutely essential work. Let’s start with introductions. Basil, you want to kick us off?

Basil Bramblebourne:
Absolutely! Basil Bramblebourne, Festival Committee. Spectacle Lane. My job is basically to walk around with a clipboard looking mildly terrified until someone tells me a problem is already solved. Which happens more than you’d think, thanks to this lot.

Pim Plumstead:
I’m Pim Plumstead, Plumstead Costumes, also on Spectacle Lane. I work with fabrics and puppets, mostly for parades and theater.

Milo Plumton:
Milo Plumton, Blue Orchard Brass Band, Munchkin Country. We play for dances, markets, anything that needs a bit of lift. And sometimes we have to figure out how to make ourselves heard over a crowd.

Julian Park:
Julian Park. Park Market Displays, Clay Hollow. I build things. Stands, frames, temporary structures. Things that need to go up, and then come down again.

Uri Pepperbourne:
Uri Pepperbourne, clinic manager at South Meadow Clinic in Quadling Country. My world is supplies and schedules, which I understand has some overlap with event planning, albeit with fewer streamers.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Perfect. Let’s dig in. Basil, you’ve been thinking ahead to winter. What’s on the horizon?

Basil Bramblebourne:
Right! So, Spectacle Lane is beautiful in snow, but it gets dark so early. I’ve proposed a neighborhood lantern walk for the deep winter months. The idea is simple: a quiet, gentle walk with lanterns. My offer to the local association is, if they can collect candle stubs—everyone has a drawer of them—I’ll source the lanterns themselves. There’s a workshop in Winkie Country that makes beautiful biodegradable paper ones. It’s not a huge festival, just a way to share some light. It’s still just a proposal, mind you. We’re working out the route and safety bits.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A lovely idea. Pim, you’re no stranger to spectacle. Can you tell us about a recent fix?

Pim Plumstead:
Of course. This was for last year’s Emerald Autumn Parade. The central dragon puppet—it’s enormous, with these great wingspan arms. During the final dress rehearsal, a seam on the leading edge of one wing tore. Not a small tear; it was threatening to run the whole length. The performance was the next day.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What did you do?

Pim Plumstead:
The outer fabric was a specific emerald brocade. I had a remnant, but sewing a new panel would have been obvious and taken hours we didn’t have. So I turned the wing inside out, cut a patch from the remnant, and stitched it behind the tear, catching the frayed edges. The patch is hidden between the outer fabric and the internal lining. From the outside, you’d never know. It held for all three performances that weekend.

Julian Park:
Smart. Invisible structure.

Pim Plumstead:
Exactly. Sometimes the best repair is the one no one sees.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Julian, you deal with visible structure. What was the last thing you built?

Julian Park:
A farmer in Clay Hollow needed a better way to sell at the cross-country market. Her old table was wobbly, and her cart was small. So I built a folding produce stand. Slatted cedar, for ventilation so her greens wouldn’t wilt. The whole thing—legs, shelves, back—folds flat. It fits perfectly in her cart, tied down with a single strap. She sets it up herself in about two minutes now.

Milo Plumton:
That’s the sort of thing. Making the practical part easy so the fun part can happen. I had a similar… not build, but a find. Our Blue Orchard Barn Dance last month. We have the band, but for announcements, thank-yous, calling the next dance set, you need a voice that carries. Our old megaphone was… sad.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What was the solution?

Milo Plumton:
The Munchkin Country Fairgrounds has a portable sound system they use for the harvest fair. I called, asked if it was idle, and arranged to borrow it. The key was testing it. I went over, checked all the cables, the battery pack, the two speakers. Made sure the microphone didn’t screech. It worked perfectly. The announcements were clear, we didn’t have to shout, and the band could just play.

Basil Bramblebourne:
See? That’s what I mean. Someone else’s problem, already solved. I’m writing that down. ‘Ask Milo about sound.’

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Uri, your world is clinics, not barn dances. But I understand logistics are universal.

Uri Pepperbourne:
They are. A recent example: we received a shipment at the clinic. It was supposed to be sterile specimen containers. The boxes arrived, but inside were… irrigation syringes. Completely wrong item, wrong supplier code on the packing slip. Our stock was going to run out in four days.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What was the process?

Uri Pepperbourne:
It was two days of phone calls. First to the delivery depot, who directed me to the regional distributor, who had to trace the order back to a central warehouse. There was a lot of holding, explaining the situation again, providing our purchase order number. Eventually, they located the correct pallet, flagged it, and put it on the next wagon going south. The right containers arrived the morning our last box was opened. It wasn’t a parade dragon, but the principle is similar: a failure in the system, patched before it becomes a crisis for the people depending on it.

Julian Park:
Mm. The patch is a phone call.

Uri Pepperbourne:
A great many phone calls, yes.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
This is a great thread. The hidden patch, the folding frame, the borrowed system, the rerouted shipment—even the planned lanterns. It’s all about anticipating the point of failure and having a material solution ready. Basil, as the proposer of new events, what do you take from this?

Basil Bramblebourne:
That I need to surround myself with people who think this way! No, seriously. My job is to hold the vision—‘wouldn’t a lantern walk be pretty?’—but the vision only happens if someone knows how to source the paper, or secure the candles, or plan a route that doesn’t get icy. Listening to this, I’m thinking the first step for the lantern walk is to find the person who’s already solved a similar problem. Maybe someone in the Reading Room Network knows about community walks. Or the Brickway Union has safety insights. The solution often exists; you just have to ask the right circle.

Milo Plumton:
That’s the melody. You hum the tune, and someone else knows the chords.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Well said. We’ve got time for a few questions from the room. Yes, in the back?
