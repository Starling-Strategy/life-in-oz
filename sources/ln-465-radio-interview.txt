Timber Verge Repair Co-op: A Chat with Bertram Meadowham

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: radio_interview
Theme: Timber Verge Repair Co-op

Host:
Welcome back to the Community Workshop, the program where we talk to the folks keeping things moving across Oz. I’m your host, and I’m very pleased to have with me today Bertram Meadowham, a bicycle mechanic with the Timber Verge Repair Co-op, calling in from Yellow Kiln. Bertram, thanks for joining us.

Bertram Meadowham:
Thank you. It’s good to be here.

Host:
Let’s start with something you’ve already done. I heard you organized a free clinic recently. Tell us about that.

Bertram Meadowham:
Yes, it was a wheel truing clinic at the community hall. The idea was simple: a lot of people here rely on bicycles, and a bent rim can sideline you. It’s a repair that looks intimidating, but the principle is straightforward. So we set up some stands, brought some tools, and I showed about a dozen folks how to use a spoke wrench, how to check for wobble and hop, and how to make small, even adjustments.

Host:
And how did that go?

Bertram Meadowham:
It went well. People were focused. You could see the moment it clicked for someone—when they turned the wrench and saw the rim move towards true. By the end, everyone had straightened at least one practice wheel. The hope is they can now fix their own, or a neighbor’s.

Host:
That’s a great skill to spread. Now, your co-op work isn’t just about teaching, it’s also hands-on repair. I understand you recently worked on a bakery’s delivery tricycle?

Bertram Meadowham:
That’s right. The Three-Loaf Bakery’s trike. It’s their main delivery vehicle, so it gets heavy daily use. The driver mentioned the chain was skipping and the gears felt vague. So I brought it into the shop.

Host:
What did you find?

Bertram Meadowham:
The chain was stretched well past its service limit—that’s what was causing the skip. The internal gear hub was just out of adjustment, which is common after miles of carrying baskets full of bread. I replaced the chain with a new one of the correct length and pitch, then cleaned and re-adjusted the hub’s shift cable and indicator mechanism.

Host:
And the outcome?

Bertram Meadowham:
It shifts cleanly now, one click per gear. The chain engages properly. The baker said it feels like a new machine, which is the goal: reliable, quiet, ready for another few thousand miles of deliveries.

Host:
From teaching a room full of people to fine-tuning a workhorse trike—it sounds like a typical week for the Timber Verge Co-op. What’s the driving force behind doing both kinds of work?

Bertram Meadowham:
Well… they’re two sides of the same thing. Fixing the trike keeps a business moving today. Teaching the clinic builds the capacity for people to keep themselves moving tomorrow. In a place like Yellow Kiln, where the Brickway is long and shops are spread out, that’s important. It’s about resilience, I suppose. Making sure a bent wheel or a worn chain isn’t the end of the trip.

Host:
Bertram Meadowham, bicycle mechanic with the Timber Verge Repair Co-op. Thank you for sharing your time and your clarity with us today.

Bertram Meadowham:
My pleasure. Thank you.
