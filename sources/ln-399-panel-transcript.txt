Gillikin Growers Circle: Finding the Fit

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Gillikin Growers

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good afternoon, everyone. I'm Rowan Bell from the Emerald City Leadership Network, and I'm pleased to be hosting this conversation with three members of our Gillikin Growers Circle. We're here to talk about the practical work of making things fit together—supplies, schedules, resources. With me are Hollis Cinderton of Cinderton Dairy in Reed Flats, Noel Banks, also from Reed Flats, and Elspeth Stoneley from the North Orchard Grain Mill. Hollis, let's start with you. The circle's been a place for shared buying for a while. Can you tell us about a recent example?

Hollis Cinderton:
Certainly. The price of good organic grain feed had been climbing, like everything else. Talking with a few others at the last meeting—Darius over at the sheep farm, the folks at Highfield—we realized we were all looking at the same supplier. So we got on the horn, put our orders together, and went back to the mill as a bloc of five. Didn't need to strong-arm anyone; the numbers did the talking. Saved each of us just shy of ten percent on that load.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A significant saving. Did it complicate the delivery or storage?

Hollis Cinderton:
A bit of a dance, yes. We had to coordinate the trucks. But we split the offloading, each taking our share straight from the wagon. Saved the driver time, too. It’s arithmetic, plain and simple. More weight in one wagon, less fuel burned overall. The hard part isn’t the math; it’s getting five farmers to agree on a Tuesday.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Noel, you’ve been involved in a different kind of coordination—sharing data. Can you tell us about that?

Noel Banks:
Yes. I maintain one of the community rain gauges for the Reed Flats area. Every morning, seven a.m., I check it and record the measurement. This past month, the data showed we’d had a steady, soft rain over three days that added up to more than the irrigation schedule assumed. I shared the weekly total with the farm irrigation group.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And what was the result of sharing that information?

Noel Banks:
The scheduler—that’s Mara—she adjusted the plan. Several farms, including Hollis’s, reduced their pumped water usage for the following week. The soil moisture was already adequate. It’s just… using what’s already there.

Hollis Cinderton:
And I thank you for it, Noel. The pump’s an old beast and doesn’t mind a rest. Saves on wear, saves on the power bill. It’s easy to just set the timer and forget it, but the numbers don’t lie.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Elspeth, turning to you at the mill. You’re often at the receiving end of these deliveries. I understand you recently faced a problem with a shipment?

Elspeth Stoneley:
Aye, we did. A wagon of barley came in from the south Quadling side late one afternoon. Driver said they’d hit a squall on the Brickway. The top layers were fair, but when we probed deeper, the grain was too damp for the silo—it would have clumped and heated in a day. Spoilage waiting to happen.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What did you do?

Elspeth Stoneley:
No time to send it back. We had the driver pull around to the loading bay, and every hand we had free started forking it out onto clean tarps we laid in the loft. It’s a big, airy space. Got it all spread thin, then set up every fan we could find—the big floor ones, the workshop ones—and ran them all night. By morning, it was dry enough to run through the cleaner and into the dry bin. Saved the whole load.

Hollis Cinderton:
That’s the difference between a miller and a middleman. A middleman would have refused the shipment, and the farmer would’ve been out a season’s work. You saw the problem and fixed it on the spot.

Elspeth Stoneley:
Well, it’s our grain too, in a manner of speaking. If it spoils, the farmer loses, and then we lose a customer. And there’s less good barley in the world, which helps no one. It was just a long, noisy night.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
These stories—shared buying, shared data, salvaging a shared resource—they all seem to hinge on seeing a connection. Hollis, what makes the Growers Circle a good place for spotting those connections?

Hollis Cinderton:
It’s the regular talking, I suppose. You hear Noel mention his rain gauge every month, so you start to think about your own water use. You hear Elspeth talk about barley quality, so you think about what you’re feeding your cows. It’s not magic. It’s just… paying attention to the person next to you, even if they’re growing something different fifty miles away.

Noel Banks:
The measurements help. They give us a common language. ‘We had twenty-five millimeters’ means the same thing to a dairy farmer, a wheat grower, and a miller.

Elspeth Stoneley:
Aye. And sometimes the connection is a wagon at your loading bay needing a dry place to sleep for the night. You don’t need a meeting to discuss that; you just need a loft and some sense.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you, all three of you, for a concrete and very useful conversation. To our listeners, if you’re involved in growing, milling, or any of the work that supports it, we invite you to explore the Gillikin Growers Circle. Details are on the Network board. Hollis, Noel, Elspeth, thank you for your time.

Hollis Cinderton:
You’re welcome.

Noel Banks:
Thank you.

Elspeth Stoneley:
Glad to.
