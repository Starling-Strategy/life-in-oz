Emerald City Leadership Network – Members' Survey
The Festival Committee is reviewing member contributions over the past year. Please describe a recent instance where you organized or adapted to ensure something worked smoothly for your community. Your response will help us understand the skills and experiences members bring to the committee.

Uri Pepperbourne:
In late spring, we identified a need for easier access to flu vaccinations in the central Quadling settlements. The South Meadow Clinic had the doses, but the travel was a barrier for many. I organized a one-day clinic at the Quadling Community Hall. The main logistical challenge was transporting the doses, which require a controlled temperature. I borrowed a refrigerated case from a supplier in Munchkinland, secured it in the back of a wagon, and managed the transport myself. We vaccinated ninety-two people that day. It was a straightforward operation, but it required precise timing and clear communication with the hall stewards to set up the intake and recovery areas efficiently.

Rosa Clay:
Adaptation is a daily practice in a ceramics studio. Just last month, I noticed one of the older kiln shelves had developed a slight warp. You can't fire a stack of pots on an unlevel surface—it risks the whole load shifting and breaking, or worse, damaging the kiln. I didn't have a replacement shelf on hand, and ordering one would have taken weeks. So, I took the shelf to the workbench, secured it, and carefully planed it down with a hand grinder until it was level again. It took the better part of an afternoon, checking and re-checking with a straightedge. It's not a glamorous story, but it prevented accidents and kept the studio's firing schedule on track for all the artists who rely on it. It's about maintaining the foundation so the creative work can happen safely.

Greer Saffroncombe:
Goldenway's cobbles were due for repair, which meant my usual route for the 47 was completely blocked for three days. The official detour would have added twelve minutes each way, which doesn't sound like much until you multiply it by thirty-odd trips a day and a bus full of people trying to get to work or appointments. I remembered a narrow service lane that runs behind the old mill—it's tight, but it's paved and connects back to the main road. I proposed it to the dispatcher, we did a test run to be sure, and we used it for the duration. We had to go slow and give way to the occasional delivery cart, but we kept the route running only about two minutes behind schedule. The passengers appreciated the consistency. Sometimes the best solution isn't the official one, it's the one that keeps things moving.
