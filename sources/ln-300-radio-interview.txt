Keeping Bees and Making Wraps in Gillikin Falls

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: radio_interview
Theme: Brookstead Apiaries

Host:
Welcome back to Community Buzz on Emerald City Radio. I’m your host, and today we’re heading north to Gillikin Falls to talk about a different kind of buzz. Ambrose Brookstead of Brookstead Apiaries is with us. Thanks for joining, Ambrose.

Ambrose Brookstead:
Thank you for having me.

Host:
Now, I hear you’ve been busy beyond the hives. You recently held a workshop in your shed?

Ambrose Brookstead:
That’s right. Just a small thing. Four folks from the area came by, all new to any of this. We spent an afternoon making beeswax wraps. You know, the cloth wraps for covering a bowl or wrapping up bread.

Host:
Using your own wax?

Ambrose Brookstead:
Yes. We melted down some clean cappings wax I’d harvested last season. It’s a good use for it. We had the cloth cut, warmed the wax in a double boiler, brushed it on, and then used a warm iron to even it out. Simple. One of the participants, she was a bit nervous about burning the cloth, but we went slow. By the end, everyone had a couple of wraps they’d made themselves to take home.

Host:
That’s a lovely, practical skill to share. What got you started offering something like that?

Ambrose Brookstead:
Well, people ask what else you can do with wax besides candles. This is a useful answer. And it’s good to see the material go straight from the hive to someone’s kitchen. Makes the connection plain.

Host:
Speaking of hives, how has the season been for you? I understand the spring had some challenges.

Ambrose Brookstead:
It did. Early spring was colder and wetter than usual. The bees stay clustered tight when it’s like that, and opening a hive in the cold rain is hard on them. So I had to postpone several inspections I had scheduled. Just pushed everything back about a week.

Host:
A whole week’s delay? That must have reshuffled your calendar quite a bit.

Ambrose Brookstead:
It did. You plan your spring work—checking food stores, looking for early signs of swarming, seeing how they came through winter. When you can’t look, you have to wait. I spent the time in the shed getting ready, cleaning equipment, things like that. The bees know their own business better than I do, anyway. They were fine once the sun came back.

Host:
So the workshop happened after that cold spell?

Ambrose Brookstead:
Yes, it was later, when things had warmed up and dried out. Better for people to travel, too. Nobody wants to drive to a shed workshop in a downpour.

Host:
A very practical point. Looking ahead, any plans for more workshops?

Ambrose Brookstead:
I’ve had a couple of people ask. Maybe another one in the fall, if there’s interest. It’s a good autumn project, when the harvesting is mostly done.

Host:
We’ll keep our listeners posted. Ambrose Brookstead of Brookstead Apiaries, thank you for the conversation and for the work you do.

Ambrose Brookstead:
My pleasure. Thank you.
