Chamber Members Put Ledgers, Loaves, and Leniency to Work

A quiet month on the Brickway often means a busy one for the Emerald City Chamber's members, whose work stitches together the practical fabric of city and country life. From balancing books to balancing acts of goodwill, several recent efforts highlight how local businesses and professionals are applying their skills within the wider community.

In the Meadowbank district, the annual community fundraiser was facing a familiar post-event headache: tallying a jumble of cash donations and pledges. Ezra Winkleley of Winkleley & Co. Accountants stepped in. "They had notes in ledgers, coins in jars, and promises on slips of paper," he said. "It was clear, but not clear enough for their report to the neighborhood council." Winkleley set up a simple, single-purpose ledger for the group, showing them how to log each donation as it came in. The result was a clean, accurate total they could present without doubt. "It took an afternoon," Winkleley noted with characteristic calm. "Now they have a method for next year, and the year after."

Down in Reed Flats, market manager Silas Hazelmere faced a different kind of calculation. A regular herb seller, whose stall is a fixture at the Reed Flats Market, arrived one morning to find their stock ruined—a burst pipe in their storage shed had flooded the drying racks. "They showed me the herbs. Soggy. Useless," Hazelmere said, his direct tone touched with weariness. The seller faced not only the loss of their goods but also the standard stall fee for the month. Hazelmere authorized a waiver. "The fee isn't the point. The point is they're here, every month, reliable. Kicking them while they're down helps no one." The seller will return next month without penalty, giving them time to recover the loss.

Back in the Emerald City, Chamber visitors from the Gillikin Growers circle got more than a standard tour of the Bell Market recently. According to several in the party, they were shepherded by Ewan Meadowton, a volunteer with the Reading Room Network, who launched into an impromptu 'memory lane' stroll. Meadowton, known for his thoughtful digressions, reportedly pointed out the exact spot where a popular pickle stall operated for decades, describing the vendor's famous garlic dills and the way the stall's green awning would flap in a south wind. The visitors, one said, appreciated the unexpected layer of history laid over the bustling modern market.

Meanwhile, an offer of clarity is on the table for Meadow Mutual Aid's sprawling network of neighborhood pods. Hal Thistleworth of Thistleworth & Co., based in Redfern Hill, has offered to create a standardized budget template. "Each pod is tracking food purchases, supply runs, and fuel costs for deliveries, but they're all doing it on different scraps of paper," Thistleworth explained in his measured way. "A simple, common template would let them see their own numbers clearly and make it easier to share information—and resources—across pods." The template, he says, is in the planning stages, awaiting feedback from the pod coordinators on what they need most to track.

And in the Junction Quarter, the morning scent of baking bread now has a different source for The Tinford Table. Owner Honor Tinford coordinated a standing order with a small local bakery for daily deliveries of sourdough loaves and rolls. "We were baking our own every morning. It was fine, but it tied up the oven and a pair of hands for two solid hours at our busiest time," Tinford said, her warm voice hinting at the relief. Securing the reliable supply means her kitchen staff can focus on prep for the day's lunch and dinner service. "The bread is excellent, the bakery gets a steady order, and we get our mornings back. It's just sensible."
