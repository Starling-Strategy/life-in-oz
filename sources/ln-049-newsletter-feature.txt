Beyond the Desk: The Quiet Work of Wizard's Office Liaisons

Theme: Wizard's Office Liaisons

When you think of the Wizard’s Office, you might picture form-stamped corridors and the distant shuffle of ledgers. But the story of Oz is also written by the employees who look up from their desks and see the city, the country, the people outside the window. The Emerald City Leadership Network’s Wizard’s Office Liaisons circle connects these public servants, and their work often bridges the gap between official record and on-the-ground reality. This month, we check in with a few members to see what that looks like.

For Abel Larkwood, a ward councillor in Bell Market, the work is fundamentally about advocacy—and navigating its setbacks. Last month, his petition for a pedestrian crossing island on the busy western approach to the market was formally declined. The Wizard’s Office cited a reallocation of the annual road safety budget.

Abel Larkwood:
The volume of cart and foot traffic on that stretch has been a concern for cycles. The data from our own counts supported the need. The decline is a setback, certainly, and I noted it as such for my constituents. It is not, however, a conclusion. The reallocation suggests priorities we must now understand before we refine the proposal.

Larkwood’s measured response—channeling disappointment into a next step—is a hallmark of the long view required in civic work.

Sometimes, the contribution is less about proposals and more about preservation. Colleagues in the Southern Survey Office mention that Nadia Stone was recently the one who noticed something missing. A concrete boundary marker, a small but critical piece of legal geography, had been displaced, apparently by forestry work in the southern timber verge.

Nadia Stone:
People said they’d seen it there for years, then it just wasn’t. It’s one of those things—you get a report saying ‘the usual marker’ is gone, but ‘usual’ isn’t a coordinate. Without the physical reference, a surveyor could be off by several paces, which in a property line or a water-rights map… well, it compounds. Reporting its absence is the first step to getting it reset.

Stone’s distrust of vague descriptions ensures the official record reflects the ground truth, not just habit.

Then there’s the work that supports the community’s memory. In the records annex, colleagues speak of Elspeth Wickham’s recent afternoon spent not on her usual filing, but on a personal mission of sorts. A local historian was researching a book on Quadling Country river settlements and needed historical floodplain maps—the kind buried in obsolete filing systems.

Elspeth Wickham:
The newer digital catalog only goes back so far. The rest is in the old cabinet indices, which are… idiosyncratic. If you don’t know the district surveyor’s numbering system from eighty cycles ago, you’ll never find the right drawer. I had a quiet afternoon, and she had a deadline. It seemed the orderly thing to do.

Wickham’s soft-spoken assistance helped pin down the precise meander of rivers long since channeled, giving the historian’s project a foundation of documented fact.

These three glimpses—a declined petition, a missing marker, a retrieved map—might not make the front page of the Gazette. But together, they sketch the profile of a vital role: the liaison who knows not just what the books say, but where the books are, what’s missing from them, and how the words inside affect the streets outside. It’s the patient, often unseen work of keeping the map aligned with the territory.
