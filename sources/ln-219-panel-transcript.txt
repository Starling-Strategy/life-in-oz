Transit Talk: Keeping the Brickway Moving

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Brickway Transit Union

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good evening everyone, and thank you for joining this Brickway Transit Union panel. I’m Rowan Bell, community manager for the network. Tonight we’re talking about the work that keeps people and goods moving. With me are Kerensa Hedgecombe, a road crew foreman from Yellow Kiln; Sable Gateby, a union steward based at the Green Market depot; and Joss Clovercombe, a bus driver from Spectacle Lane. Let’s start with you, Kerensa. We had some heavy weather in late winter. What does that mean for the roads you work on?

Kerensa Hedgecombe:
Means a lot of mud and a few surprises. Take the Western Route past the old clearing. That bank on the north side’s always been a bit soft. After the last big storm, it decided to slide right down, took out about fifteen feet of the shoulder and was starting to creep into the slow lane. You could see the cracks from the road.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
So what was the response?

Kerensa Hedgecombe:
We got the call at first light. Had a crew of six out there by mid-morning. Couldn’t just pile more dirt on it; it’d just slide again. We had to dig back to stable ground, lay in a proper gravel base, and then reinforce it with timber cribbing. Took us the better part of three days in the drizzle. But we got both lanes clear and stable for the trucks. They were backed up using just the eastbound lane for a day and a half, grumbling the whole time. They were happier once we were done.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
That’s a concrete result. Sable, shifting from the road to the depot. Your work often involves the conditions inside. Can you give us an example?

Sable Gateby:
Certainly. My focus is making sure the contract we negotiated is actually lived out on the ground. It’s not just about wages. It’s about dignity and having the tools to do the job safely and efficiently. A recent issue was in the Green Market tram depot crew room. Twelve lockers were broken—doors hanging off, locks jammed, interiors rusted out. People were having to change in the open or leave their personal gear on benches. It was unacceptable.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What did you do?

Sable Gateby:
I filed a formal grievance, citing Article Seven, Section Two: ‘The employer shall provide adequate and secure facilities for the storage of employee personal effects.’ I presented it with photos and a list of affected crew. Sometimes these things get bogged down in ‘we’ll look into the budget.’ I made it clear this was a clear violation and a safety issue. They approved the repair order within the week. The new lockers were installed last month.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A good use of the contract. Joss, your world is the bus itself, and the passengers. Sometimes the service you provide goes beyond the route map.

Joss Clovercombe:
It does. You see a lot of things left behind. Books, umbrellas, lunch pails. Most of it ends up in the lost-and-found. But a couple weeks back, I was doing my end-of-line sweep on the 42, and on the very back seat was this parcel, wrapped in brown paper. Not huge. I picked it up and it was soft.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
What was it?

Joss Clovercombe:
I opened the paper a bit to see if there was a name. It was knitting wool, a whole lot of skeins of this bright, butter-yellow color. Beautiful stuff. No tag, no note inside. Now, wool like that isn’t cheap, and someone had put work into collecting it. I logged it at the depot, but I also left a note on the board describing it. Standard procedure is to hold items for seven days, but I kept an eye out for it. Two days later, this older woman comes in, nearly in tears, asking if anyone had seen her yellow wool. She’d bought it in Gillikin for a grandchild’s blanket and thought she’d left it on a bench in the market. I fetched it for her. The relief on her face… that was worth the little bit of extra looking.

Kerensa Hedgecombe:
See, that’s the thing. It’s all connected. We fix the road so Joss’s bus—and everyone else—has a smooth ride. Sable makes sure the depot isn’t falling apart so folks start their shift right. And Joss here looks out for the people on the bus. It’s a system.

Sable Gateby:
Exactly. And the system works when every part is respected and maintained. The lockers might seem small, but when crew feel their workplace respects them, it shows in how they deal with the public. It’s all part of the service.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
We have a question from the audience, submitted earlier: ‘With the spring thaw, what’s the biggest concern on the Winkie Country routes?’ Kerensa?

Kerensa Hedgecombe:
Potholes. No contest. The freeze and thaw cycles just chew up the asphalt, especially on the older sections near the kilns. We’ve got a patching crew going constantly, but it’s stopgap. The real need is a full resurface on about five miles of the Kiln Access Road. That’s a budget fight for another day.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And a question for Sable: ‘Are there other facility issues you’re currently addressing?’

Sable Gateby:
Yes. The ventilation in the older tram cabs is next on my list. Drivers on the long routes through the city are reporting it’s inadequate, especially in summer. I’m gathering reports now to present a case for an inspection and upgrade schedule. It’s a planned action, not yet happened.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you. Joss, a final one for you: ‘What’s the most common lost item?’

Joss Clovercombe:
Gloves. Single gloves, mostly. Left or right, doesn’t matter. We have a bin full of them at the depot. Never a matching pair.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A mystery for the ages. Thank you all three for your time and for the vital work you do. This has been a Brickway Transit Union panel. Goodnight.
