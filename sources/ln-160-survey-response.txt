Emerald City Leadership Network – Quarterly Members' Survey (January 2025)

1. In your own words, what is the core purpose of the Wizard's Office Liaisons circle?
2. Describe a recent instance where you used your role or skills to move a project or issue forward.
3. How has being part of this circle changed the way you approach your work?
4. What is one thing our circle could do to be more effective?

Willa Rushwick:
1. To act as a two-way conduit. We take the practical, on-the-ground realities from the network's circles and translate them into the specific procedures and forms of the Wizard's Office, and vice-versa. It's about making civic machinery legible and usable.
2. For about six weeks, I logged seventeen separate reports from residents and a few shopkeepers about a persistent pothole on Spectacle Lane. Each one came in through a different channel—a call, a handwritten note, even a sketch left at the desk. The key was tracking them all under a single case file, which finally showed a clear pattern of repeated complaints. That file got the repair crew scheduled for next week.
3. I'm much more systematic now. I see the value in consistent logging, even for seemingly minor things. What looks like an isolated report to one person is often part of a larger pattern visible only from a central desk.
4. Regular, brief check-ins with specific circles. A monthly fifteen-minute call with, say, the Brickway Transit Union could flag procedural snags before they become major delays.

Delphine Saffronmere:
1. To bridge the gap between policy and people. The Office has rules and processes; our communities have needs and deadlines. We help them understand each other.
2. Nothing grand, but I designed simple weekly planners for my tutoring students—just fill-in-the-blank sheets with slots for assignments, reading, and review. It's helped them structure their independent study time, which was a constant struggle. They're meeting their deadlines more consistently now, which means less last-minute panic for everyone.
3. It's reinforced a pragmatic approach. Instead of seeing a rule as a barrier, I now look for the simplest, most straightforward path within it to get a good outcome. Clarity first.
4. We could create a shared, simple glossary of common Office terms and acronyms. Something a road crew member or a grower could glance at and understand what 'RFQ' or 'docket review' actually means for their timeline.

Cyrus Spindlefield:
1. To ensure continuity and accuracy. Decisions made yesterday affect work today. Our role is to maintain the thread, to find the precedent, so that current actions are informed and correct.
2. The Brickway Works crew needed the original load rating reports for the Bluebell Crossing bridge for a weight restriction review. The digital record only went back twenty years. I spent three afternoons in the basement archives, following the old filing system. I found the paper reports from forty years ago, which confirmed the original specifications they needed.
3. I am more persistent in seeking primary sources. The most recent summary is not always the full story. Digging deeper, even when it's inconvenient, prevents assumptions that could lead to error.
4. A shared, secure digital log for tracking ongoing liaison requests, even the small ones. It would prevent duplication of effort and show us where bottlenecks are forming.

Edmund Tinworth:
1. To provide the documentary foundation for fair process. When a hearing officer or a committee makes a decision, they must have the complete history of relevant past decisions at hand. We assemble that history.
2. For the upcoming Brickway Transit Union hearing on the proposed route change, the hearing officer needed all prior rulings on similar petitions. I prepared a comprehensive archival folder, pulling the decisions, the maps submitted at the time, and the public comment summaries from three previous cases over the last decade. It saved the officer several hours of preparatory work.
3. I think more in terms of complete sets now. It's not just about finding one document; it's about anticipating the entire constellation of related materials someone might need to see the full picture.
4. We could standardize the format for these decision histories. A consistent table of contents and a brief cover summary would make them instantly more useful, regardless of which liaison compiles them.
