Plain Words and Public Works: A Liaison Panel

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Wizard's Office Liaisons

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Welcome, everyone. I’m Rowan Bell, community manager for the Emerald City Leadership Network. Today’s conversation is with members of our Wizard’s Office Liaisons circle. We’ll talk about the day-to-day, the tangible projects, and how we communicate about them. Our panelists: Tilda Stoneford, public works engineer; Ozma Tipwell, civics teacher and youth-league organizer from Westgate Mills; and Orla Greenwick, public notices editor. Let’s start with a simple question: what does a successful project look like from your side of things?

Tilda Stoneford:
From public works, it’s when the system functions as it should again, and the disruption is minimal. Last week, a playground drain in the Gate Quarter was completely blocked—silt, leaf debris, the usual autumn accumulation that stayed put. We brought in a portable vacuum truck. The crew cleared the pipe in about three hours, and the playground was usable the same afternoon. The success was in the coordination: getting the right equipment to the right place without closing the entire park.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And the follow-up? Does the Wizard’s Office track that?

Tilda Stoneford:
We schedule a re-check in two weeks. For that playground, we’ll look for sediment build-up again. It’s a known spot. The record is internal, but if a pattern emerges, we might propose a redesign to the planning group. That’s a longer process.

Ozma Tipwell:
I love that—showing the working. For my students, success looks different. It’s the bench they can sit on. We had this neglected pocket park in Westgate Mills, just a patch of grass and a tired tree. The league secured a small materials grant from the Wizard’s Office—for lumber and brackets, nothing fancy. Then we built two benches. Simple, sturdy, painted a sort of cheerful blue. The installation day was the real lesson: measuring twice, using a post-hole digger, checking for level. Now they walk by and see something they made being used by neighbors. That’s the civics.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Orla, your work comes after the fact, in a way. How do you see success?

Orla Greenwick:
Clarity. When a notice about a road closure or a park improvement is understood on the first reading. The public doesn’t need to decipher it. My role is to take the technical information—like Tilda’s drain clearance or the granting of funds like Ozma’s—and render it into plain, actionable language for posting on the civic boards and the official digest.

Ozma Tipwell:
Which can be a trick! I remember our grant approval notice. It started with a string of codes. Orla’s team turned it into ‘Funding awarded for Westgate Mills youth park project.’ Much better.

Orla Greenwick:
Precisely. And that leads me to a plan I have. I intend to offer a workshop for Liaisons on writing public notices in plain language. We’d use real examples from past postings—some that worked well, some that were… less clear. The goal is to help everyone, at the project origin point, think about how the public will receive the information.

Tilda Stoneford:
That would be valuable. My reports are necessarily technical. A bridge between that jargon and the public notice would streamline the process. I’d attend.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Let’s talk about that bridge. Tilda, when you finish a job like the drain, what information do you pass along, and to whom?

Tilda Stoneford:
A work completion form goes to my supervisor and the assets ledger. It lists the location, crew hours, equipment used, and any follow-up scheduled. That’s the record. Separately, if the work required a temporary closure, we’d have sent an alert to the notices office—Orla’s domain—ahead of time. After, we might send a brief ‘restored’ notice, but often the public sees the barrier tape gone and that’s signal enough.

Orla Greenwick:
The ‘restored’ notices are underused, in my opinion. A simple ‘Playground drain clearing complete’ posted on the local board manages expectations. It tells people we know it was an issue and it’s been resolved. It closes the loop.

Ozma Tipwell:
My students would have loved to see a notice about their benches! ‘New seating installed by Westgate Mills Youth League.’ It reinforces the connection. Could we do that for small projects?

Orla Greenwick:
It’s possible. The constraint is volume. But a standardized template for community-led improvements, filed by the sponsoring office… that’s an idea worth discussing after the workshop. The key is the initial write-up being clear.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Ozma, getting that grant—was the process clear?

Ozma Tipwell:
The forms were straightforward, once we found the right one. It was a ‘Small Public Spaces’ grant. The language was plain: ‘Describe the project,’ ‘List materials needed,’ ‘Who benefits.’ No legalisms. I think that’s why we got it. The officer could picture the benches.

Tilda Stoneford:
That’s the ideal. My budget requests for equipment sometimes disappear into a fog of specifications. If the person reading it can’t picture the vacuum truck on a muddy lawn, the need seems abstract.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
So the skill of picturing something—making it concrete—is what links your work, all three. Orla’s workshop seems to sit right at that junction.

Orla Greenwick:
Yes. It’s not about dumbing things down. It’s about precision in common language. ‘Remove silt and leaf debris’ instead of ‘Perform hydrological obstruction remediation.’ One tells you what we did; the other tells you nothing.

Ozma Tipwell:
Show your working, but in words.

Tilda Stoneford:
Agreed. And the result of good communication is less frustration all around. Fewer calls to the office asking when the playground puddle will drain.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
We’re out of time. Thank you, Tilda, Ozma, Orla. For those listening, Orla will circulate a note about the planned workshop. This is the kind of cross-circle sharing the network exists for. Safe travels home.
