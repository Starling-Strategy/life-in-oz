Festival Committee Roundtable: Making the Work a Party

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: panel_transcript
Theme: Festival Committee

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good afternoon, everyone, and welcome to this Festival Committee chat. I'm Rowan Bell, the community manager for the Emerald City Leadership Network, and I'm really pleased to be here with three folks who make our celebrations—both big and small—happen. We've got Basil Bramblebourne, events producer for the Festival Committee; Rosa Clay, ceramics instructor from Clay Hollow; and Mabry Rushcombe, costume maker extraordinaire. The idea today is just to talk about the work, the little triumphs, and how we keep the energy going. Basil, let's start with you. I heard you recently wrapped up the volunteer thank-you.

Basil Bramblebourne:
Oh, yes! We did. Oh, it was such a relief. After the Autumn Lights procession, the committee was just... spent. So I thought, we can't just send a note. I booked the Gillikin Riverside landing—you know, that little cleared space where the ferries used to dock—and told everyone it was a bring-your-own-dish potluck. The key was I didn't ask them to bring plates. I'd borrowed this whole trolley-load of mismatched crockery from that old tearoom on Porcelain Row that closed last season. Cups, saucers, little sandwich plates, all different patterns. It was chaotic and charming. Everyone spent the first ten minutes just picking out their favorite set. It made it feel like a proper party, not just another meeting with food.

Rosa Clay:
That's wonderful. A clean surface to start from, even if it's a picnic blanket. It shows care.

Basil Bramblebourne:
Exactly! And it was so low-pressure. I just provided the spot and the things to eat off of. The volunteers brought this incredible spread. A Winkie spiced stew, Gillikin apple tarts, those little Quadling pepper pies... We ate, we watched the river, we didn't talk about logistics once. Well, maybe once. But only briefly!

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Rosa, turning to your corner of the world. Your community tile project for the Clay Hollow festival has become a bit of a legend. Can you tell us how that worked?

Rosa Clay:
It's simple, really. We set up long tables in the square with bags of prepared clay. I showed everyone how to wedge it properly—no air bubbles, that's crucial—and roll it out to a uniform thickness. Then they could take a tile, about the size of their palm, and decorate it. We had slips, carving tools, stamps. No rules, just their mark. A parent and child made matching suns. An older gentleman painstakingly carved the outline of his old rowboat. The key was having a clear end point. After the festival, I bisque-fired them all, glazed them with a clear coat for durability, and mounted them on a large cedar board. It's now on the wall of our community hall. It’s not a mural by one artist; it’s a conversation by a whole town. You have to give people a firm foundation and clean tools, and they’ll build something beautiful.

Mabry Rushcombe:
Oh, I love that! It’s like costuming a crowd scene, but permanent. Each little piece has its own character, but together they make a splendid whole. Texture! Color! It must be dazzling in the light.

Rosa Clay:
It is. And it was manageable. We did it over two afternoons. No one got overwhelmed.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Mabry, you're nodding so hard. Your work often involves sourcing very specific materials. Does that community, hands-on feeling translate when you're hunting for, say, feathers?

Mabry Rushcombe:
In a different way, yes! It's about connection, but further afield. I was commissioned to create headpieces for that new opera production at the Amphitheatre—very grand, very 'Winds of the North' sort of theme. They needed volume, they needed drama, they needed lightness. Goose and pheasant feathers were perfect. But I didn't want just any feathers from a warehouse. I spent weeks writing letters—actual letters—to suppliers in Winkie Country. I found a family-run operation that collects molted feathers from their own flocks and from carefully managed wild roosts. No harm to the birds. When the box arrived... oh, Rowan, you should have seen it. It was like opening a cloud. Downy soft goose feathers in drifts, and these incredible, iridescent pheasant plumes with eyes like copper and emerald. Sourcing it this way, it felt right. It gave the project a story before I even sewed the first stitch. The singers will wear a piece of the Winkie countryside on their heads.

Basil Bramblebourne:
That’s it! That’s the feeling. Whether it’s a borrowed teacup, a community tile, or a ethically sourced feather. It’s about adding that layer of thought. It turns a task into a gift. My volunteers felt thanked because of the silly plates. Your tiles gave people a permanent place in the festival. And your feathers, Mabry, they carry the whole ethos of the production.

Mabry Rushcombe:
Yes! And it's practical, too. These feathers are sturdier. They haven't been stressed. They'll hold up through every performance.

Rosa Clay:
A good wedge of clay, properly prepared, won't crack in the kiln. Fundamentals support the flourish.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
It sounds like a common thread is intentional sourcing—of objects, of materials, of goodwill. Not just using what's easiest, but what adds meaning. Does that ever feel like extra work? Basil, hunting down a tearoom's worth of china?

Basil Bramblebourne:
Oh, constantly. I had to haul that trolley myself! But the payoff... Seeing someone's face light up because they got the cup with the little blue violets? That’s the fuel. It’s the difference between a meeting and a memory.

Mabry Rushcombe:
And my correspondence with the feather family! I must have written five drafts of the first letter. But now I have a supplier I trust, and the opera company has a line in the program notes about sustainable sourcing. It becomes part of the art.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Rosa, final word?

Rosa Clay:
It’s not extra work. It’s the work. The clean wedge, the right clay, the clear invitation. It’s what allows the community’s contribution to shine. Without that, you just have a mess. With it, you have a wall full of stories.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you, all three. To everyone listening, you can find links to the Festival Committee’s upcoming planning meetings on the network board. Thanks for making the work a party.
