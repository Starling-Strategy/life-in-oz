A Warm Room, a Voice Archive, and a Loaf of Bread: Meadow Mutual Aid on Community Radio

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: radio_interview
Theme: Meadow Mutual Aid

Host:
Good morning, listeners. You're tuned in to Community Currents on the Emerald City public airwaves. I'm your host, and today we're continuing our series on the Emerald City Leadership Network and the work of its member circles. In the studio with me is Briony Gateholt, who leads the Reed Flats warm room as part of Meadow Mutual Aid. Welcome, Briony.

Briony Gateholt:
Thanks for having me. It's good to be here.

Host:
And joining us by phone from Green Market is Nan Kilnholt, a volunteer with the network's Reading Room oral history project. Hello, Nan.

Nan Kilnholt:
Hello there. Thank you for the invitation.

Host:
Briony, let's start with you. For those who don't know, what does the Reed Flats warm room do?

Briony Gateholt:
It's a simple thing, really. We open a community space on the colder afternoons and evenings. It's a place to get out of the chill, have a hot drink, maybe a bit of food, and sit with neighbors. It's about basic warmth—both the kind from a stove and the kind from company.

Host:
And I understand you've recently secured a new source for food?

Briony Gateholt:
That's right. I went and talked to the baker over at the shop just off the Brickway, you know the one with the blue door? We worked out that they'd set aside their day-old loaves for us twice a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays. It's nothing fancy, but it's reliable. It means we can always offer toast, or a simple bread-and-broth meal. It takes one worry off the list.

Host:
That's a wonderful example of local connection. Nan, your work with the Reading Room Network is quite different, preserving stories. Can you tell us about your project?

Nan Kilnholt:
Certainly. We've been collecting memories from older residents about how things used to be. My recent task was a series of interviews about the old ferry that used to cross the Munchkin River at the shallows, before the low bridge was built. People would talk about the ferryman, the fare, what they were carrying across—sometimes livestock, sometimes market goods.

Host:
And you transcribed these?

Nan Kilnholt:
I did. Hours of listening and typing, getting the phrases just as they were said. Then I wrote up a summary of the common threads—the schedules, the sounds of the pulley system, the way the river would rise in spring. It's all typed up now for the network's archive. It's not a grand history book, but it holds the voices.

Host:
Briony, does that kind of archival work connect to the immediate needs you see in the warm room?

Briony Gateholt:
In a way, yes. Nan's work is about making sure we remember where we came from, the fabric of the place. The warm room is about holding the fabric of the community together right now. They're different looms, but they're weaving the same cloth, if that makes sense. We need both the bread for today and the stories from yesterday.

Nan Kilnholt:
Oh, I like that. And you know, some of the people who come to share their stories with me… they might also find themselves needing a warm room on a bitter afternoon. Dignity is in being heard, and it's in being warm. They're not so far apart.

Host:
That's a powerful thought. Briony, what's next for the warm room?

Briony Gateholt:
Keeping the stove lit, frankly. Making sure our volunteers are supported. The bread is a big help, but we're always looking for more volunteers for the evening shifts as the nights get longer.

Host:
And Nan, what stories are you listening for next?

Nan Kilnholt:
I've had a few people mention the old method of signaling between farms in the valley before we had the bell system. I think that might be my next thread to pull on. The ways we used to call out to each other.

Host:
Briony Gateholt and Nan Kilnholt, thank you both for your time and for your work.

Briony Gateholt:
Thank you.

Nan Kilnholt:
My pleasure.
