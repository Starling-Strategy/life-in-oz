Brickway to Clinic: What's Making Work Better?

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: radio_interview
Theme: Quadling Health Access

Host:
Good morning, Oz. Welcome back to ‘Making It Work’ on the Quadling Community Radio service. I’m your host, coming to you from a very foggy Hammerhead Rise. Today, we’re talking about the small changes that make big differences in our day-to-day jobs. My guest is Bea Wickton, patient transport coordinator with Quadling Health Access. And joining us from the Brickway Depot in Bluebell Crossing, we have Doris Plumbourne, a depot mechanic for Brickway Transit. Welcome to you both.

Bea Wickton:
Thanks for having me.

Doris Plumbourne:
Morning.

Host:
Bea, let’s start with you. Your role at Quadling Health Access has you seeing a lot of our community members, often in stressful situations. I understand you recently finished a project looking at the patient experience. Can you tell us about that?

Bea Wickton:
Yes, of course. It wasn't anything grand, but it felt necessary. For the last six months, I kept a notebook by my desk. Whenever a patient or a family member waiting for transport made a comment about the clinic waiting areas—too cold, too loud, the chairs were hard, things like that—I’d jot it down. Last month, I gathered all those notes and summarized them into a short report for our management.

Host:
What did you find?

Bea Wickton:
The main themes were about comfort and information. People sitting for long periods, especially our older visitors or those recovering from procedures, found the benches difficult. And several people mentioned they weren’t sure where to find a drink of water or how long a delay might be. I just organized the comments by frequency and passed it along. They’ve acknowledged it and said they’ll review the furniture budget.

Host:
A concrete step from gathered voices. Doris, you’re on the other side of that coin—you’re thinking about comfort for the workers. What’s on your mind at the depot?

Doris Plumbourne:
Well, it’s the break room. We spend eight, ten hours a day on concrete. Your knees talk to you. Your back talks to you. We’ve got these old wooden chairs in there that might as well be rocks. I’m proposing the depot purchase two proper ergonomic chairs. The kind with some support. You get off your feet for fifteen minutes, you should actually get some relief, not just trade one ache for another.

Host:
Is this an official proposal now?

Doris Plumbourne:
It’s in the works. I’ve mentioned it to our shop steward. The next step is getting a couple of model numbers and prices, then putting it to the union circle. It’s not a flashy fix, but it’s a real one. Two chairs. That’s my plan.

Host:
Bea, hearing Doris talk about a plan for physical relief, does that connect to what you heard from patients?

Bea Wickton:
Absolutely. It’s the same principle, just different settings. Whether you’re waiting for a clinic appointment or for your shift to end, your environment either adds to the strain or eases it. Doris is being very methodical—gathering the need, finding a specific solution. That’s how my report started, just paying attention to the repeated comments.

Doris Plumbourne:
Methodical. I like that. Mostly I just call it not wanting to be sore all the time. But yeah. You see a problem, you find a fix. Even a small one.

Host:
Small fixes that make the workday sustainable. Doris Plumbourne in Bluebell Crossing, thank you. Bea Wickton here in Hammerhead Rise, thank you. We’ll take a short break. When we come back, the week’s weather along the Brickway and across the marshlands. You’re listening to ‘Making It Work.’
