Small Repairs, Big Difference: How Emerald City Network Members Keep Things Running

EMERALD CITY—June 2026. While grand projects capture headlines, the steady work of maintenance, repair, and careful organization is what keeps a community humming. This week, members of the Emerald City Leadership Network have been busy with precisely that kind of essential, everyday work.

In Lantern Row, school librarian Ursa Saffronridge brought a piece of Quadling Country to her students. She coordinated a virtual author visit for several classrooms, using the school’s projector system to facilitate a lively Q&A session with a children’s book writer from across the Gillikin border. “The children had their questions written down on little slips of paper,” Saffronridge said. “Seeing an author’s face, hearing their voice—it makes the stories real for them. The projector meant everyone in the library could see.” The session ended with a promise from the author to send a signed copy of their latest book for the school’s collection.

Meanwhile, in South Copse, Quadling Country, electrician Dov Quadford was dealing with a more tangible kind of connection. Called to an old farmhouse, he identified faulty grounding in the kitchen wiring, a situation he described as “a pot of water waiting to boil over.” He prevented a potential shock hazard and recommended a full panel upgrade to the homeowners. “The old cloth-wrapped stuff was just done for,” Quadford said bluntly. “You can’t put a bandage on that. I showed them where the danger was, gave them the estimate for the new panel, and they signed on the spot. Safer for them, safer for me to work on it later.”

Over in Blue Orchard, Winkie Country, a different kind of structural assessment was taking place. According to market organizers, Jude Quadstead was called to assess the bent frame of a wind-damaged community market shelter. After a close inspection, Quadstead reportedly recommended a complete rebuild rather than a patch job, arguing that welding the bent supports would only create weak points for the next storm. The market committee is now reviewing his written assessment as they seek funding for the new structure.

Back in Munchkin Country, in Meadowbank, Cassian Amberford was implementing a system of a different scale. At Amberford Pharmacy, he set up a simple card-file system to track and send postcard reminders for regular prescription refills to over thirty patients with chronic conditions. “It’s not fancy,” Amberford explained in his calm, methodical tone. “But for folks who might forget in the bustle of the week, or who don’t like the automated phone calls, a colorful postcard in the mail is a gentle nudge. We’ve already had several people come in saying, ‘Oh, yes, I saw your card.’ It keeps them on schedule.”

Not far away, in Reed Flats, Kai Tinmere was ensuring a different kind of schedule was kept. As the keeper of the Reed Flats community fridge, Tinmere set up a weekly bicycle collection of day-old bread from a bakery on Tinsmith Row. “It’s a win-win,” Tinmere said casually. “The bakery doesn’t like to waste it, and a loaf of bread is a solid base for a meal. It’s become a reliable staple. People know if they come by on a Tuesday afternoon, there’ll be fresh bread.” The collection now adds several dozen loaves to the fridge’s offerings each week.
