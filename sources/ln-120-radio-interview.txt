Voices from the North: Squirrels, Rain, and the Work of Keeping Things Running

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: radio_interview
Theme: Gillikin Growers

Host:
Good afternoon, listeners. This is the Northern Voices hour on the Gillikin Community Radio Network. I’m your host. For our segment today, we’re joined by a member of the Gillikin Growers circle, Merritt Meadowfield, up at the North Orchard Grain Mill. Merritt, thanks for coming on.

Merritt Meadowfield:
Happy to be here.

Host:
And we also have Noel Banks on the line from Reed Flats, down in Munchkin Country. Noel, hello.

Noel Banks:
Hello. Yes.

Host:
You’re a farmhand, and you also volunteer with the rain-gauge network. Let’s start with you, actually. We had some fierce winds a few nights back. Did that affect your readings?

Noel Banks:
It did. The gauge is mounted at the edge of the lower field, near a line of poplars. After that wind, before dawn, I went out with my lantern. The funnel was completely blocked. Leaves, small twigs, even a bit of chaff. If I hadn’t cleared it, the next day’s rain—it was 0.8 inches—would have been diverted, pooled on top of the debris. The measurement would have been inaccurate.

Host:
So a simple act of maintenance.

Noel Banks:
Simple, but necessary. The Reed Flats readings go to the Growers circle and the Meadow Mutual Aid folks for their irrigation schedules. An error of even a few tenths of an inch can mean overwatering for some plots. It’s just part of the routine.

Host:
Merritt, speaking of routine maintenance, I understand you’ve had some… uninvited guests at the mill?

Merritt Meadowfield:
You could say that. Squirrels. Persistent little engineers. They’d found a way into the primary storage silo—a small gap where a vent pipe meets the roof. They weren’t eating much, but they were… nesting. Which meant…

Host:
Contamination.

Merritt Meadowfield:
Exactly. We’d lose small batches, twenty, thirty pounds at a time. Not a crisis, but a consistent nuisance. And we didn’t want to harm them. So, I spent a few evenings in the workshop. The solution was a series of internal baffles—angled sheets of metal—that break up the open space right below the entry point. Makes it feel unstable, not like a nice hollow tree. Then, over the exterior gap, a fine mesh screen, spaced out about an inch. They can’t chew through it to get a foothold, and they can’t get past the baffles inside. It’s been three weeks now. No new nests, no contaminated grain.

Noel Banks:
That’s a clever design. Non-lethal. Was the mesh galvanized?

Merritt Meadowfield:
It is. Has to withstand our weather. The baffles are just scrap sheet metal from an old hopper we decommissioned last year.

Host:
So it’s about adapting what you have. Noel, does that resonate with your work?

Noel Banks:
In a way. My tool is a pair of tweezers and a cloth to clear the gauge. Merritt’s are metal shears and a welder. But the principle is observation, then a measured response. You see a problem that disrupts the system—blocked data, spoiled grain—and you apply a specific fix. No drama. Just the work.

Merritt Meadowfield:
(dryly) Well, I wouldn’t say no drama. You should’ve seen the look on the head miller’s face when I explained why I was welding scrap metal inside the premium wheat silo. But he came around when the spoilage stopped.

Host:
And that’s it for today’s Northern Voices. Thank you both, Merritt Meadowfield of the North Orchard Mill and Noel Banks in Reed Flats, for sharing the work.

Merritt Meadowfield:
Thank you.

Noel Banks:
Goodbye.
