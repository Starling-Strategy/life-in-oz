Timber Verge Repair Co-op: A Webinar on Skills in the Community

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: webinar
Theme: Timber Verge Repair Co-op

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Okay, I think we’re live. Welcome, everyone. I’m Rowan Bell, the community manager for the Emerald City Leadership Network. Thanks for logging in on a Tuesday evening. This is one of our series of conversations with members of our network’s circles, and tonight we’re talking with three members of the Timber Verge Repair Co-op. The idea here is just to hear a bit about the work they do and how it shows up in their communities. It’s not a formal presentation. I’ve got Jude Quadstead from Westgate Mills in the Winkie Country, Dov Quadford from South Copse in the Quadling Country, and Kai Tinmere from Reed Flats in Munchkin Country. Welcome, all.

Jude Quadstead:
Thanks, Rowan.

Dov Quadford:
Evening.

Kai Tinmere:
Hey, good to see some familiar icons here.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Good. Jude, maybe we can start with you. I understand you do fabrication and welding. Could you tell us about a recent project?

Jude Quadstead:
Sure. An apple orchard down in Munchkin Country needed a new picking ladder. Their land is on a slope, and the trees are… compact. A standard ladder wouldn’t do. They needed something tall but narrow, and it had to tuck away in their equipment trailer when they moved between groves.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Compact trees, that checks out. So what did you build?

Jude Quadstead:
Built an eight-footer. Steel frame, with a hinge point about halfway up. The top section folds down flat against the lower legs. When it’s folded, it’s about as thick as my forearm and just over eight feet long. Slides right into the side of their trailer. Took a couple of days to get the pivot pins right so it’s solid when it’s up but still easy for one person to manage.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And it’s in use now?

Jude Quadstead:
It is. Got a note last week. They’re using it for the early varieties. Said it’s steady on the uneven ground. That’s the main thing.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Excellent. Thank you. Dov, you’re an electrician. I’ve heard a story from your neighborhood, but I’d like you to tell it. Something about an oven?

Dov Quadford:
Right. My neighbor—older woman, lives two houses down—her oven stopped heating. She mentioned it to another neighbor, who mentioned it to me. I went over to take a look. The heating element had cracked. It was an older model, not something you can just pick up at a supplier anymore.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
So what was the solution?

Dov Quadford:
I had an old unit of roughly the same make in my workshop, taken out of a remodel last year. The element in it was still good. Pulled it, tested it, fit it into hers. Took about an hour. She offered to pay, but it was just an hour and a part I wasn’t using. Told her to forget it.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
That’s a good bit of resourcefulness. Kai, your work is a bit different. You keep a community fridge in Reed Flats. Can you tell us about a recent improvement there?

Kai Tinmere:
Yeah, absolutely. The fridge is on the pavement outside the old community hall. Works great, but when it rains, which it does, people are standing there getting soaked while they’re looking through it or waiting for someone else. Not great. We had an old market stall awning that got retired when the vendor upgraded. Big piece of green canvas, still waterproof, with the grommets all intact.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
And you turned it into a shelter?

Kai Tinmere:
Exactly. Got some poles from the same vendor, lashed them to the fence posts on either side of the pavement. Threw the canvas over, tied it down tight. It’s not fancy, but it covers the area right in front of the fridge and gives about six feet of dry standing room. Went up in an afternoon. The next time it drizzled, I saw a parent with a little kid under there, completely dry, taking their time. That was the point.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
That’s a great use of what was already around. Jude, Dov—does that kind of repurposing resonate with your work?

Dov Quadford:
Constantly. Half the job is knowing what you can salvage and what you can’t. That oven element is a small example. Sometimes it’s conduit, sometimes it’s a fixture. If it’s safe and it works, why melt it down and start over?

Jude Quadstead:
Same. I used scrap steel for the ladder bracing. Not the main frame, but the cross-supports. It’s cleaned, it’s strong. Saves them money, saves material. It’s just sense.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Kai, your project required less technical skill but perhaps more community coordination. How did you get the awning?

Kai Tinmere:
I just asked. The vendor at the weekly market knew me from the fridge—we take their unsold produce sometimes. When they got a new one, the old one was rolled up in their storage shed. I said, ‘If you’re just storing it, can we have it for the fridge spot?’ They said yes before I finished the sentence. A lot of it is just letting people know what you need.

Dov Quadford:
That’s it. The neighbor didn’t call me directly. It went through two people. If folks don’t know what you can do, they won’t ask.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Which is part of why the co-op and this network exist. We’ve got a few minutes left. Any final thoughts from each of you on what ‘repair’ means in your context?

Jude Quadstead:
Making a thing fit the need. Not just fixing what’s broken, but building what’s missing so it lasts.

Dov Quadford:
Getting something working again, safely, without creating a burden for the person who needs it.

Kai Tinmere:
For me, it’s fixing a gap in the system. The fridge fixes a food access gap. The awning fixed a comfort gap. It’s all repair, just not always with a wrench.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Well said. Thank you all for your time and your words. Very much appreciated. And thank you to everyone listening. The recording and notes will be in the network digest later this week. Goodnight.

Kai Tinmere:
Night, everyone.

Dov Quadford:
Take care.

Jude Quadstead:
Bye.
