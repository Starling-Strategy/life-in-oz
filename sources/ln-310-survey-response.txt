Gillikin Growers Circle Members' Survey Responses

What is one piece of work from the past year you're particularly glad you got done?

Barnaby Haybourne:
Oh, the Winkie Works Guild asked for help with their rooftop garden—those containers on the old brickworks. They had some sad-looking lettuces. I advised them on a soil mix: two parts screened compost, one part gritty sand from the riverbank, and a half-part of that fine, aged bark we get from the Timber Verge. Light enough for the roof, but it holds moisture just right. They sent me a picture last week; the herbs are thriving. It’s a good reminder that soil isn't just for fields.

Elspeth Stoneley:
The secondary grinding unit at the mill threw its drive belt. The part they wanted to order was quoted at a silly price and would’ve taken weeks. I measured the old one, walked down to the harness maker in town, and we figured out a custom-sized leather belt would do the trick. I fitted it myself that afternoon. It’s running smooth as anything now. Saved the order cost and didn’t lose a day of milling for the local growers.

Cassian Wheatbourne:
Grafted a wild pear. Found a scraggly old tree up in the rocky hills last fall, took some scion wood. Grafted it onto a dwarfing rootstock in the nursery row. Took, and the new growth came through that nasty late frost we had in April with hardly a shiver. The other young trees got nipped, but this one didn't blink. It’s a tough little thing. Might have something special there in a few years.

Nimmie Meadowbright:
The cooperative needed clover seed for five different plots. I got the quantities from each member, combined them into one bulk order, and negotiated with the supplier. We saved fifteen percent off the individual prices. The discount notice is on the treasurer's report, and the seed was delivered and divided without issue.

What’s a challenge in your work right now that other members might have ideas about?

Barnaby Haybourne:
Testing soil amendments without breaking the bank. I've got a theory about using ground oyster-shell from the Quadling coast to adjust pH in our heavier clay soils, but getting a consistent, affordable supply shipped here is the trick. Has anyone tried a similar material, or know a carrier who brings loads north regularly?

Elspeth Stoneley:
Keeping the knowledge for these old machines in more than one head. I can keep ours running with a bit of leather and ingenuity, but if I take a holiday, the whole place holds its breath. We need a simple manual, with diagrams, for the basic repairs. I’m no artist, though.

Cassian Wheatbourne:
Marketing a small, unusual crop. If this new pear variety pans out, it won't be a cartload for the general market. It’d be for folk who want something different. How do you find those buyers without spending all your time at a stall in the city? Do the specialty grocers in the Emerald City take on tiny lots?

Nimmie Meadowbright:
Record-keeping for shared equipment. The cooperative owns a seed drill and a small thresher. The logbook for who has it and its condition is... informal. I believe a clear, simple system is needed, but it must be easy for everyone to use, not just those fond of receipts. I would welcome examples of what works for other circles.

What does being part of the Growers Circle bring to your work?

Barnaby Haybourne:
Connections! It’s one thing to talk soil science with another grower, but the chance to advise the brickworks guild? That came through the network. It broadens the mind. You remember you’re solving problems for a whole city, not just a plot.

Elspeth Stoneley:
A sense of scale. I’m in the mill all day. Hearing from Cassian about his pears or Nimmie about her co-op’s purchases… it reminds me what’s at the end of the process. The grain isn’t just grain; it’s someone’s bread, someone’s harvest. Keeps the work grounded.

Cassian Wheatbourne:
Soundboards. When you’re out in the orchard by yourself, you can turn an idea over in your head forever. Mention it to Barnaby or Elspeth after a meeting, and you’ll get a practical thought you hadn’t considered. Saves time and money.

Nimmie Meadowbright:
Efficiency. The bulk seed purchase is a direct example. By pooling our needs through the circle’s connections, we achieved a financial saving for all parties. It is a practical application of collective bargaining that benefits independent operations.
