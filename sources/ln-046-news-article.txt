The Depot That Keeps Munchkin Country Moving

SYNTHETIC SOURCE — original fictional text; no real event or publication.
Format: news_article
Theme: Brickway Junction depot

The afternoon sun slants through the tall windows of the Brickway Junction depot, painting warm rectangles on the worn floorboards. Inside, the air hums with the low murmur of travelers checking schedules, the rustle of paper, and the distant rumble of a bus pulling into its bay. For decades, this building has been the beating heart of transport for central Munchkin Country, where the paved Brickway meets the smaller roads to villages like Cornfield and Meadow Mirth. Yet lately, a quiet transformation has taken root behind the main inquiry counter, one that has travelers leaving not just with a ticket, but with a plan.

It started a few years back, when Dorothy Gale—then a depot assistant—noticed a pattern. 'People would come in looking a bit lost,' she recalls. 'They'd missed their bus, or the one they needed wasn't running that day, and you could see the worry. They just wanted to know how to get home.'

Dorothy Gale:
The timetables were all there on the wall, but if you don't know the system, it's just a grid of numbers. So I made a different list. For every village on the network, I wrote down the next three buses going that way, and the connections you'd need if you'd missed the direct one. Like, if the 10:15 to the Emerald City is gone, here's how to get to Crossroads and catch the 11:05 from there. I checked every entry against the master schedule. The goal was simple: no one walks away empty-handed.

That list became the 'getting home' sheet, a single page of clear, destination-focused instructions. Gale typed it up, had it laminated, and propped it next to the timetable. The effect was immediate. Instead of poring over columns, a flustered traveler could point to their village and be handed a pre-written route. The sheet has been updated seasonally ever since, its corners now soft from use.

The influence of this simple tool has rippled outward from the depot's inquiry desk. Mina Vale, who has driven Route 4 between Brickway Junction and the Gillikin border for fourteen years, sees it in her passengers' hands.

Mina Vale:
Oh, sure. I'll pull up at the stop by the old mill, and someone's there waiting, holding that sheet. They give it a little tap like, 'I'm prepared.' They know they're on the right bus because it says so right there. Saves me explaining the route five times a trip, which is lovely, because my timetable is tight—we've got to keep to the bricks, you know.

Perhaps the most telling adoption, however, is next door at the Brickway Junction parcel depot. Clerk Quentin Birch, whose domain is a landscape of stacked boxes and the persistent challenge of faded address labels, decided the sheet was too useful to stay in the bus hall.

Quentin Birch:
We get a lot of the same crowd in here—drivers picking up parts, growers sending samples, folks collecting orders. And they'd always be asking me, 'Quentin, when's the next bus to so-and-so?' as if I run the transit authority from behind a pile of parcels. So I asked for some copies. Now I keep a stack right here on the counter, next to the customs forms. It cuts down on the questions, and honestly, it's just good service. If someone's worried about missing their connection, they're not really listening to me explain where their missing label might have gone.

The sheet itself is a model of utilitarian clarity. Villages are listed alphabetically. Under 'Meadow Mirth,' it reads: 'Direct: 12:45, 3:10, 5:30. If missed: Take 1:05 to Cornfield, connect to 1:40 shuttle.' There are no flourishes, no promotional slogans—just information, tested and verified.

Community manager Rowan Bell of the Emerald City Leadership Network, which counts Gale, Vale, and Birch among its members, sees the project as something more fundamental than a schedule aid. 'It's about dignity,' Bell notes. 'It treats people's time, and their need to get home, as seriously as the system itself does. It turns a moment of confusion into a moment of competence.'

Back at the depot, the shift changes. A new assistant takes her place behind the counter, and a traveler approaches, looking uncertain. The assistant doesn't reach for the master timetable. She smiles, touches the laminated sheet, and asks, 'Where are you headed today?'
