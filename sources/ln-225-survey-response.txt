Quadling Health Access Members' Survey, April 2025.

Thank you for your time. Your insights help us understand what's working, what's challenging, and how we can better support each other's efforts.

Question 1: Looking back over the last few months, what piece of work are you most glad you saw through to completion?

Ewan Stoneworth:
We had about two dozen families relocating to Violet Hills from our catchment area. The continuity of care for chronic conditions was a real concern. I arranged for a secure hand-courier—someone we trust—to transfer a full box of archived patient records to the new clinic there. It was just a box, but it meant those families didn't have to start from scratch, explaining their histories all over again. I'm glad it's done.

Question 2: What has been the most persistent or surprising obstacle you've navigated recently?

Jago Winkleholt:
The unpredictability of the routes. Just last week, a key bridge on the Clay Hollow route was closed for an unscheduled safety inspection. No warning. I had an entire morning's worth of patient pickups scheduled, all timed to clinic appointments in the city. I spent an hour on the community board and my handheld, replanning everything: calling patients, finding alternate drivers who knew the back ways, rescheduling the ones we couldn't reach in time. The obstacle is never the transport itself; it's the ground shifting under you.

Question 3: Describe a recent collaboration—with another network circle or an outside group—that felt particularly effective.

Odile Pepperby:
We have several residents with low vision who miss keeping up with the news. I reached out to Meadow Mutual Aid to see if they had a volunteer with a good reading voice. They connected us with a retired teacher who now visits every Marketday afternoon. She reads the week's papers aloud in the sunroom. It's become quite a social event; others drop in to listen. Hardly headline news, but it fostered a new connection that feels solid and kind.

Question 4: What's a routine part of your work that you wish others in the network better understood?

Odile Wickley:
The logistics of a simple-sounding task: getting someone to an appointment. It's not just booking a time. Recently, I scheduled back-to-back hearing aid assessments for three North Orchard seniors at the same Emerald City clinic. Then came the real work: coordinating their availability, finding a minibus driver willing to take a longer route to collect all three, ensuring the clinic knew they'd arrive together, confirming with family members. It's a puzzle where every piece has to move at the right time, and if one piece is late, the whole picture falls apart. That methodical coordination is the bulk of the job.

Question 5: Looking ahead, what kind of support from the broader Emerald City Leadership Network would make your work more sustainable?

Ewan Stoneworth:
A shared, secure registry for road closures and infrastructure work across the counties. Jago's bridge problem is my problem next week if a courier route is blocked. If we all had a clearer, faster way to report and see those disruptions, we could plan around them instead of reacting in a panic.

Jago Winkleholt:
What Ewan said. A reliable, up-to-date map of passable routes would save hours of stress every week.

Odile Pepperby:
More connections like the one with Meadow Mutual Aid. A simple directory of which circles offer what—reading volunteers, meal delivery, minor home repairs—would help us solve problems without starting a new search from scratch every time.

Odile Wickley:
Agreed. A network is only as strong as its shared knowledge. A way to easily share these logistical templates—like how to set up a group transport—would prevent each of us from reinventing the wheel.
