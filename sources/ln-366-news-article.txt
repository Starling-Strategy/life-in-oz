Small Circle, Big Moves: Upholsterers, Laundries, and a Librarian Make Their Own Luck

EMERALD CITY – In an economy where every stitch and every mile counts, members of the Emerald City Leadership Network’s Small Business Circle are finding that the old ways still hold value—especially when shared with a neighbor. From the quiet corners of Quadling Country to the bustling lanes of Emerald City, a series of small, concrete actions is helping businesses save money, preserve skills, and serve their communities a little better.

In the Winkie Works Guild hall last month, the rhythmic thump-thump-thump of a mallet setting springs into webbing was a lesson in frugal ingenuity. Yves Brambleholt of Brambleholt Upholstery in South Meadow led a workshop for eight guild members on tying coil springs, a fundamental skill for furniture restoration.

Yves Brambleholt:
The tool doesn't have to be expensive to be true. I built this jig from scrap pine and some twine. It holds the spring base steady so you can focus on the knot tension. Too loose, and the chair will sag in a season. Too tight, and you risk breaking the twine or the spring. It's a feel you develop.

One attendee, a furniture repair apprentice, said the workshop saved her the cost of a commercial jig and gave her the confidence to take on a sprung sofa repair she'd been avoiding.

Meanwhile, in Blue Orchard, Munchkin Country, Golda Hedgestead of Hedgestead Upholstery was applying a similar reverence for materials and history. The Blue Orchard library's reading nook features four wing chairs, their original leather covers worn thin but still beautiful. The library had hoped for a repair, not a replacement.

Golda Hedgestead:
The horsehair stuffing was mostly intact, just compacted. I teased it apart, added a little fresh wool for support, and saved nearly all of it. Re-attaching the original covers was fiddly work—the leather is fragile—but now they have their chairs back, with all the character they've accumulated. It felt right.

Over in the laundry business, the calculus is often one of logistics. Rhoda Reedshaw of Reedshaw Laundry in Hammerhead Rise found her daily delivery route was being eaten away by a long-term street repair project on Cobble Lane.

Rhoda Reedshaw:
I was adding twenty minutes and a lot of pothole-dodging every day. I finally called the Brickway Transit Union's route planning line. The fellow there looked at my stops and sketched out an alternate loop in two minutes flat. We avoid Cobble Lane entirely now. The van's suspension thanks me, and I get home for dinner.

Inspired by efficiency, Fergus Bramblemore of Bramblemore Laundry in Reed Flats is looking to go a step further. He's in talks with a nearby baker and a hardware store about a shared delivery solution.

Fergus Bramblemore:
We all make runs out to the outlying farms—me for linens, them for bread orders and tool deliveries. If we split the cost of a dedicated van and a driver for one long loop every Tuesday, we each cut our vehicle costs by two-thirds. The baker likes the idea; I'm meeting the hardware owner next week. It's about seeing the map as one big route, not three little ones.

Back in the Emerald City, the past is providing its own kind of map. At the Green Market Reading Room, librarian Vida Barleystead recently concluded a month-long display titled "Where We Traded."

Vida Barleystead:
I borrowed foam boards from the framer down the lane. We mounted historic trade directories and maps of the market districts—showing where the tanners, chandlers, and cloth merchants were clustered. It wasn't just nostalgia. Several small business owners came in and said it helped them think about location, foot traffic, and suppliers in a new way. One said, 'So that's why that alley is called Needle's Eye.'

These five stories—a spring-tying jig, a careful restoration, a rerouted van, a proposed shared route, and a historical display—share no single industry or location. What they share is a mindset: a willingness to look closely at a problem, to value craft over flash, and to believe that the solution might already be in the community, waiting to be tied together.
