Emerald City Leadership Network – Members' Survey (Festival Committee Focus)

Question 1: In the last year, what’s one piece of work you’re proud of that involved careful planning or adaptation?

Mabry Brickford:
It was the bus map for the new Quadling health centre. The schedules from the outlying hamlets were all on different posters, different formats. It just made it hard for people to plan a visit. I sat down with all the timetables, mapped out the connections, and put it all on one poster—arrival times to the centre, departure times back home. We got them printed and up in every community hall from Redfern Hill out to the Glade. It’s not flashy, but it’s one less thing for someone to figure out when they’re already worried about getting to an appointment.

Ansel Wickby:
Had a customer with a twenty-year-old wall oven. The thermostat failed. Nothing modern fits it. Told them straight: it’ll be out for about three weeks while I find the part. Had to write to a specialist supplier over in the Gillikin highlands, wait for the post, then calibrate it once it arrived. It’s planning for the wait, you see. Setting the expectation, then following the trail until the job’s done right. They were patient, and the oven works like new now.

Aurelia Ashmere:
A leaky pipe in a storage closet at school dampened a whole cart of old geography textbooks. Rather than see them tossed, I spread them out on tables in the reading room, used fans for circulation, and air-dried every page. Once they were dry, I spent a few evenings after school rebinding the ones that were coming apart. They’re back in circulation now. It was about adapting the space and the process to save what we had.

Flora Hayworth:
The Small Business Circle’s fiftieth-anniversary dinner. They wanted a traditional feel, roast goose, but the rented loft kitchen only had domestic ovens. So I borrowed a second one from a friend, arranged them side-by-side, and timed the roasting of two whole Quadling geese perfectly so they’d rest together. It was a bit of a dance, managing the heat and the space, but it worked. The smell alone was worth the planning.

Question 2: For the upcoming network festival, what kind of role or task would feel most natural to you?

Mabry Brickford:
Something logistical. Helping people figure out how to get there, or mapping out where things are on the grounds. I’m good at making complex information clear and accessible.

Ansel Wickby:
Setting up or troubleshooting any equipment. Pop-up tents, signage, that sort of thing. Or being a point person for any last-minute repairs. Keep things running.

Aurelia Ashmere:
Organizing a schedule for talks or demonstrations, or perhaps a quiet reading or storytelling corner for families. Something that brings a little order and a welcoming space.

Flora Hayworth:
The food, naturally! But specifically, coordinating the local vendors or helping organize a community potluck area. Making sure there’s good, practical food for everyone and that the folks serving it have what they need.

Question 3: What’s one thing you hope the festival achieves for the wider Oz community?

Mabry Brickford:
A sense of connection that outlasts the day. That someone from Timber Verge meets someone from Reed Flats and they realize they’ve been solving similar problems, and maybe they keep talking after.

Ansel Wickby:
To show that good work is often slow work. It’s not all instant. That it’s okay to take the time to find the right part, or the right person.

Aurelia Ashmere:
I hope it feels genuinely welcoming to everyone, especially people who might not usually come to big gatherings. A place where you can participate or just watch, and either is fine.

Flora Hayworth:
A shared meal, or even just the sight and smell of food being prepared together. It’s a universal language. I hope people leave feeling like they shared a table, even briefly, with neighbors they didn’t know they had.
