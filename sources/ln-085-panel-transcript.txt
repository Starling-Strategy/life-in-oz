Theme: Wizard's Office Liaisons

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Welcome, everyone. I'm Rowan Bell from the Emerald City Leadership Network. Thank you for making time tonight. Our topic is how our members who work as Wizard's Office Liaisons connect the Office's work to the streets and the schools. We have four members with us. Ozma Tipwell, a civics teacher from Westgate Mills. Abel Larkwood, a ward councillor from Bell Market. Zora Oakholt, a records clerk. And Magnus Greendale, the public notices editor. Let's start with a broad question for you all. What does being a 'liaison' mean in your day-to-day?

Ozma Tipwell:
For me, it's a two-way street. I'm taking the structure of how our city works—budgets, committees, public notices—and showing my students the working parts. And then I'm taking what I hear in the halls and cafeterias back to the right desk at the Office. It's not a formal title; it's just paying attention in both directions.

Abel Larkwood:
I would concur. The formal role is councillor, but the liaison function is implicit. It is the translation of constituent concerns into actionable administrative items, and then the translation of administrative timelines and constraints back to the constituents. The goal is clarity and managed expectations.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Ozma, you had a concrete project recently that fits that. The school supply drive?

Ozma Tipwell:
Yes. The start-of-term support closet was looking thin. Instead of just telling the students it was a problem, we made it a civics exercise. We set up collection bins in the main hall and talked about mutual aid as a community function. They responded wonderfully. We gathered enough notebooks, pencils, and rulers to fill fifty complete packages. It showed them that filling a gap doesn't always require a petition to the Office; sometimes it's about organizing what's already in the room.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Abel, your work often starts with a report to the Office. Can you walk us through an example?

Abel Larkwood:
Certainly. A persistent issue was a deep pothole on a side street near Bell Market. Vehicle damage reports had been filed, but the repair schedule was backed up. The liaison work was in the follow-up. I arranged for a set of temporary traffic cones from the local depot to be placed around it. A simple, physical intervention. It prevented further damage and signaled that the report was seen, even if the permanent fix was still some days out. The repair crew has since been scheduled.

Zora Oakholt:
The cones... they are a visual flag. Like the reminder mailing. I work with the data, the renewals. I saw a pattern. Late business license renewals, clustered in one Emerald City precinct. Not scattered, a cluster. So I prompted a targeted reminder mailing to that area. Three steps: identify the pattern, draft the notice, coordinate the mailing. It is more effective than a city-wide notice.

Magnus Greendale:
Ah, the notices. That's my world. Zora's pattern-spotting creates the 'why' for a mailing. My job is to make sure the 'what' is understood. I had a notice about Mill Stream dredging from the engineering office. Densely technical. Full of cubic-yard estimates and regulatory citations. Important for the businesses on the banks, but incomprehensible. I redrafted it. Plain language posters: 'The stream will be lower and noisier from these dates. Access to your rear loading dock will shift here.' Understood. That's the goal.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Magnus, when you get something from engineering, what's the first thing you do?

Magnus Greendale:
I sigh. Then I read it and ask three questions: Who absolutely needs to know this? What do they need to do? And what will they see or hear? The original notice answered none of those. It was a record of compliance for a file. My rewrite tried to answer all three. The posters went up, and we didn't get a single call asking what 'sediment mitigation' meant. We got calls about specific parking alterations, which we could answer. That's success.

Ozma Tipwell:
That's what I tell my students! Show your working. If the answer is 'move your truck,' don't bury it in five paragraphs of why. Magnus, you'd be a great guest speaker.

Magnus Greendale:
I'm not sure I'm that exciting, but I could talk about passive voice and why we avoid it.

Abel Larkwood:
The principle is sound. Clarity prevents secondary problems. Unclear dredging notices lead to blocked access, which leads to delivery delays, which leads to business complaints to my office. A clear notice at the start short-circuits that chain.

Zora Oakholt:
Patterns, cones, plain language. They are all forms of a signpost. Directing attention. Preventing the wrong turn.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
A question for the group. Where does this liaison work usually break down? What's the common snag?

Magnus Greendale:
Assumption. Assuming people know which department does what. Assuming they'll read past the first line. Assuming a poster on a kiosk is enough without also telling the local councilor or the school principal.

Abel Larkwood:
The silo. A matter is considered resolved when it leaves one department's desk, without confirmation it has landed on the right one. The pothole was 'reported to Public Works.' But the cones were in a Transport Safety depot. Different silo. The liaison has to walk between them.

Ozma Tipwell:
For me, it's time. Or the lack of it. I have my classroom duties first. The liaison work happens in the margins. Setting up the supply bins was a lunchtime project. It's worth it, but it relies on people being willing to use their margins.

Zora Oakholt:
The data is not always connected. Renewals here, complaints there. My pattern was only visible because I cross-reference two lists. Not all the lists talk to each other.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you. That's a useful set of challenges. Final thought from each of you: one thing you wish more people knew about the Wizard's Office.

Ozma Tipwell:
That it's full of people like Zora and Magnus. Not wizards, just people with jobs, trying to make things run.

Abel Larkwood:
That it operates on procedures. Those can be slow, but they are also what prevents favoritism. The liaison helps navigate the procedure.

Zora Oakholt:
The records... they are not just for filing. They are for finding. The pattern is in there, if you look.

Magnus Greendale:
That we want the notices to be read. Truly. We are not trying to hide things in small print. We are just often drowning in the technical details from the experts. Help us by telling us when it's still not clear.

Rowan Bell — community manager:
Thank you all. That was excellent. We'll leave it there for tonight.
