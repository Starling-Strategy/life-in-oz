Emerald City Leadership Network Members' Survey – Gillikin Growers Circle
The following are compiled responses from Gillikin Growers members to our annual survey, conducted in December 2025.

Question 1: What is one recent, specific project or effort you've undertaken within your community or business?

Henry Dale:
Well, we had a little problem with rodents showing too much interest in one of the South Meadow community compost bins. It was getting into things it shouldn't. So I spent a few days adjusting the feedstock mix—more browns, less of the really attractive kitchen scraps—and then I topped the whole thing with a good thick layer of coarse woody material. Makes it less of a cozy home. The activity dropped off noticeably within a week. It's just about maintaining the right balance.

Edith Reedstead:
The mill's dust filtration is showing its age. It's a practical necessity. I'm drafting a proposal to the Emerald City Chamber to see if we can organize a shared fundraiser with some of the other small mills and bakeries in the region. It's a sizable piece of equipment, and doing it together seems the sensible way.

Ambrose Brookstead:
The craft group in Gillikin Falls was putting together candles for their winter festival fundraiser. They needed clean wax. I had several pounds of filtered beeswax from the last harvest that was just sitting, waiting for the right use. I donated it to them. It saved them the cost of materials, and the candles turned out beautifully. A good use for what we have.

Posy Oakdale:
Last fall, I ran an experiment with a new compost blend. I mixed spent coffee grounds from the Heatherford reading room with leaf mulch collected from the park maintenance crews. It created this rich, crumbly mixture, like a dark cake for the soil. I worked it into my squash beds over the winter. The following season, the yield was significantly improved—the vines were stronger, and the fruits set more reliably. It felt like giving the plants a steady, long-lasting meal.

Question 2: What is the biggest challenge facing growers or producers in your area right now?

Henry Dale:
Education, consistently. It's not just about putting scraps in a bin. It's understanding what happens next. When people know why a mix matters, or what 'finished' compost looks and smells like, they become stewards, not just users. Getting that knowledge to stick is the ongoing work.

Edith Reedstead:
The cost and upkeep of essential machinery. It's the backbone of what we do, turning grain into flour. When a key system like filtration or the stones themselves needs work, it's not a small bill. Finding ways to share that burden or plan for it collectively is the challenge.

Ambrose Brookstead:
Unpredictable weather patterns. The bees depend on a certain rhythm of bloom and temperature. When springs are too wet or too cold, or summers too dry, it disrupts everything. It makes planning the apiary's movement and harvest a much more careful guessing game.

Posy Oakdale:
Access to consistent, affordable soil amendments on a larger scale. My little experiment worked wonderfully, but scaling that up for market production is different. Sourcing bulk materials that are clean and reliable, without breaking the bank, is a constant puzzle. It's like trying to find the right perennial for a tricky spot—you have to keep testing.

Question 3: What kind of support from the wider Network would be most useful to you?

Henry Dale:
Connections to community centers or the Reading Room Network. A place to host a short, hands-on workshop now and then would be ideal. I can bring the bins and the samples.

Edith Reedstead:
Introductions. If the Chamber circle has members with experience in equipment grants or collaborative purchasing, a conversation would be valuable. I have the draft proposal; I need the right ears to hear it.

Ambrose Brookstead:
Maybe a forum to share observations. If other growers are tracking bloom times or pest appearances, comparing notes across the counties could help us all adapt. A simple, regular check-in.

Posy Oakdale:
A materials exchange log? Somewhere to note surplus or needs. I often have extra seed starts, or need more straw for mulch. If others have coffee grounds or leaves, and I have plants... it seems a natural way to share resources, like a guild planting where everything supports everything else.
