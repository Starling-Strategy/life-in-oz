Iris,

I wanted to share what worked out with the relief cook last week, since we talked about finding ways to reduce the interruptions during service.

I wrote down the exact sequence and quantities for the roast chicken dish. It’s a staple, so the cook already knew how to prepare it, but the specific timing for the vegetables and the sauce was a bit loose in my head. I printed the sheet and taped it to the prep station.

During the trial run, she followed the sheet without calling me away from the purchasing ledger. That was the main goal. I was able to finish the supplier order list without being pulled into the kitchen every ten minutes. She managed the usual batch size perfectly. The second seasonal dish, the beet salad, remains undocumented, but we decided not to overcomplicate things for now. The chicken sheet is enough for this rotation.

That sounds like a solid win. Keeping the kitchen flow steady is half the battle. It’s interesting how much just having the numbers written down changes the dynamic, even when the cook knows the food.

I’ve been trying a similar approach with the home-care schedule. I’ve set up specific callback windows for helpers whose paid shifts make repeated daytime calls difficult. I only record the availability needed for scheduling, not personal details. It keeps the log clean.

I completed the confirmations for next week during those windows. It went smoothly. I did leave one request visibly pending on the board, though. I couldn’t match it with an available helper, and I didn’t want to promise a visit that I couldn’t guarantee. Better to have it sitting there than to have a client waiting for a confirmation that never comes.
