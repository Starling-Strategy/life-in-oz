Chamber Briefs: Market Tech, Stationery Pop-up, and Insurance Guidance

The Emerald City Chamber's summer slate is buzzing with activity, from practical deals for market vendors to creative pop-ups and essential advice for new businesses. Here's a look at what members have been up to.

Zeb Lanternham, the Chamber's Membership officer, recently finalized an agreement that directly benefits vendors at markets across the city. By negotiating a bulk discount with a payment technology provider, he secured a lower per-unit price on a popular model of contactless card reader terminal for any Chamber member.

Zeb Lanternham:
The goal was straightforward: reduce the upfront cost for our members who rely on quick, reliable transactions. For a flower seller at the Brickway market or a jeweler at the Quadling craft fair, saving even a modest amount on the terminal means more working capital for their stock.

A vendor at the Emerald City Farmers Market, who purchased one of the discounted terminals, confirmed the savings covered the cost of her market stall fee for the week.

Meanwhile, Verity Meadowford of Meadowford Stationers is taking her business to the people. She plans to set up a weekend pop-up stall at the Emerald City Farmers Market later this season.

Verity Meadowford:
I want to showcase our seed paper notebooks and the drawing sets made with vegetable-based inks right where people are thinking about growth and nature. It's a different feeling than browsing in the shop on Spectacle Lane. I'm hoping to talk to customers, see what they're drawn to, and maybe sketch a few ideas for new designs based on what I hear.

Meadowford's pop-up remains in the planning stages, with dates to be announced.

In a quieter but equally critical corner of commerce, Golda Hedgeby of Hedgeby Insurance provided some crucial clarity for a new entrepreneur. A food truck owner, newly arrived in the Emerald City and applying for his first festival permit, was under the impression his vehicle insurance covered all his business risks.

Golda Hedgeby:
He had the right policy for the truck itself, which is great. But when you're serving food to the public, you need a separate product liability layer. The festival application specifically asked for it, and he didn't have it. It's a common point of confusion. We reviewed his documents, and he was able to add the coverage before submitting his permit, so there was no delay.

The food truck, specializing in Winkie-style flatbreads, has since been approved to operate at the upcoming Festival of the Four Corners.

These efforts—a negotiated discount, a planned market presence, and one-on-one guidance—highlight the Chamber's role in connecting the practical dots of city business, from the technological to the tactile to the contractual.
